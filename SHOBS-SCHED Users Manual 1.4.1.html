1. What SHOBS-SCHED is

SHOBS-SCHED, the Shiloh Hill Observatory Photometry Scheduler, is a desktop program that plans your nights of photometry: exoplanet transits, variable stars, and campaigns (stars or fields you come back to night after night). It plans tonight (the default) or up to seven nights ahead, prints a plan card you can keep at the telescope, and writes each night's target list for ASIAIR or NINA.

You do not need to be a programmer to use it. If you know your site, your telescope and your camera, you can plan a night with it.

A photometry scheduler, not an imaging planner

SHOBS-SCHED plans for measuring stars, not for making pictures of them. That changes what a good plan looks like, and the program is built around it:

  • Transits need baseline. A transit is only useful with steady out-of-transit light before ingress and after egress, so a transit block runs from well before ingress to well after egress, and the program checks that the whole transit happens in the dark and above your limits.
  • Some events happen at a set time. An eclipsing binary's minimum or an RR Lyrae star's maximum happens when it happens. SHOBS-SCHED places these fixed-time targets first and fits everything else around them.
  • Exposures must stay linear. A star whose brightest pixel goes past the camera's linear limit gives bad photometry. SHOBS-SCHED chooses an exposure for each target that keeps the peak under your linear limit, and warns you when one of your own exposures may saturate (the verdict then says Saturates).
  • Know before you go. For each target SHOBS-SCHED works out the precision your telescope and camera can reach, and the S/N of the transit or of the star's light changes, with a one-word verdict: Detectable, Marginal or Out of reach. With a SHOBS-FLUX profile of your system the numbers are measured; without one they are rough estimates, and the word carries a "?".
  • Meridian flips matter. A flip in the middle of a transit costs frames and changes the star's place on the sensor. On a German equatorial mount the planner times flips so that, where it can, the flip happens before the run starts. A flexible block (a campaign or an any-time variable) that fits wholly before its meridian runs there instead, with no flip at all, as long as that does not push another target out.
  • Campaigns have goals. A star you follow for weeks gets a goal such as "three nights a week", and the planner pushes it up when it falls behind.

What it does not do:

  • It does not plan deep-sky imaging. It has no idea of "hours of integration per filter" on a nebula or galaxy. General imaging planners do that job better.
  • It does not run your camera or mount. It writes a plan; ASIAIR, NINA or your capture program runs the night. The ASIAIR and NINA files carry the targets' positions only; times, exposures and notes stay on the plan card.
  • It does not reduce your frames. That is SHOBS-P's job (below).
  • It needs the internet for lookups and the weather. Target lookups (NASA Exoplanet Archive, AAVSO VSX, VizieR, SIMBAD) and the forecast (Open-Meteo) need a connection. When SIMBAD or VizieR does not answer, a mirror of it is asked; when nothing answers, saved copies are used. The plan itself is worked out on your computer.

The window

SHOBS-SCHED has one main window. The blue sidebar on the left reads Shiloh Hill / Observatory / Photometry / Scheduler, with (SHOBS-SCHED) under it and the line Plans each night's photometry: transits, variable stars and campaigns. Below that are the four steps, and at the bottom Credits and sources and the version number (Version 1.4.1).

The white area on the right is the page you are working on. Every page has its title at the top and a short line under it saying what the page is for.

Along the bottom runs the status strip. Its first line holds the latest news (for example ✔ Setup saved.); its second line is a short credits line. While the program is working, a green busy bar appears at the lower left of the status strip; chapter 8 explains it.

SHOBS-SCHED always opens on the Setup page.

The four pages

You work through the steps in the sidebar from top to bottom. Once Setup is done, you will mostly live on pages 2 to 4.

Step Page What you do there
1 Setup Enter your site, time zone, telescope, camera, mount, limits, horizon and weather source, once, and the filters the Find buttons use and the darks you already have.
2 Targets Keep your list of what you want to measure: Campaigns, Transits and Variables. Tick Plan for the ones to schedule. Find suggested transits and variables for the coming nights; ticking a suggestion keeps it.
3 Schedule Build the nights. Each night gets a tab with a night plot, the nightly table, flats and darks, what didn't fit and why, and free time. Mark what happened afterwards.
4 OBSPlan The plan card on screen. Print it, or send one night at a time to ASIAIR or NINA.

Every page is covered in Part 2 of this manual.

Colors

Each target type has its own color, used for its blocks on the night plot, its rows in the nightly table and its cells on the plan card:

Type Color

Every final action button (Build schedule, Add, Print plan card, Save and so on) is green and dims briefly when you press it. Every message carries an icon as well as a color: ✔ blue for normal news, ⚠ dark amber on pale yellow for a caution, ✖ red when something did not work.

The site's clock

Every time SHOBS-SCHED shows is on your site's clock, in the site's time zone from the Setup page, whatever time zone the computer itself is set to. Labels say which clock that is, for example Local time (UTC−4). On a night when daylight saving starts or ends, the label shows both offsets: Local time (UTC−4 → UTC−5).

This matters if you plan for a remote observatory, or travel with a laptop: the plan always shows the times you will see on a clock at the telescope.

A "night" is named by the date it starts on. The night of Tuesday 20 October runs from Tuesday evening to Wednesday morning, and its morning hours (01:00, 04:30) belong to it.

SHOBS-SCHED and SHOBS-P

SHOBS-SCHED's sister program, SHOBS-P, reduces the frames you take: it turns a night of FITS frames into a light curve or a transit fit. SHOBS-SCHED plans the night; SHOBS-P measures it.

  • SHOBS-SCHED works on its own. You do not need SHOBS-P installed. Type your site and equipment on the Setup page and everything works.
  • When SHOBS-P is installed on the same computer, SHOBS-SCHED reads SHOBS-P's settings when it starts, so you type your site and equipment only once. It reads your latitude, longitude, elevation, time zone, aperture, focal length, pixel size, telescope name, linear limit, mount type and observer code. It also takes the limits from SHOBS-P's Tonight's transits finder (minimum altitude, darkness, and the baseline, brightness and depth filter) as starting values, once.
  • It never changes SHOBS-P's files. Each program owns its own settings.
  • Every Setup value shows where it came from, with a small gray tag beside the field: from SHOBS-P, from FITS or set here. A value you type in SHOBS-SCHED wins over SHOBS-P's from then on. Chapter 4 explains the tags.

How to use this manual

  • Part 1 (chapters 1 to 3) gets you installed and through your first night.
  • Part 2 (chapters 4 to 7) covers each page in turn, every field and button.
  • Part 3 is reference: the busy bar and messages, credits, troubleshooting, and a glossary.

Button names and other words you see on the screen are printed in bold, exactly as the program shows them. Messages are printed in italics. Examples use a made-up "Example Observatory" at latitude 40.0° N, longitude 100.0° W, with a 0.25 m telescope.

Tip: If you are in a hurry, go straight to 3. Your first night, step by step. It takes you from an empty Setup page to a printed plan card.

2. Installing and starting

SHOBS-SCHED is a Python program. You install Python once, unzip SHOBS-SCHED, and run one file that sets everything else up. It takes a few minutes, most of it waiting for downloads.

What you need

  • Windows 10 or 11, Linux, or a Mac. SHOBS-SCHED comes with start files for all three. The Linux start file has been tested on Linux. The Mac start files are new in 1.2.0 and have not yet been tested on a Mac by a user (see "Linux and the Mac" below).
  • Python 3.11 or newer, with Tkinter (the window toolkit). Python from python.org includes it. On Windows, Anaconda or Miniconda also works.
  • An internet connection, for the install and later for target lookups and the weather.

SHOBS-SCHED uses only three packages on top of Python: NumPy, tzdata (the world's time zones, so your site's daylight-saving changes are right on every computer) and Matplotlib (the night plot). The setup installs them for you.

Install Python (Windows)

  1. Go to python.org/downloads and download the latest Python for Windows.
  2. Run the installer. On the first screen, tick Add python.exe to PATH. The SHOBS-SCHED start files find Python this way.
  3. Click Install Now and wait for it to finish.

Tip: If you already have Python 3.11 or newer (for SHOBS-P, for example), you can skip this. SHOBS-SCHED tries each Python it finds and uses the first one that works, Tkinter included. Your Python itself is left as it is.

Install SHOBS-SCHED on Windows

  1. Unzip SHOBS-SCHED somewhere permanent, for example C:\Astro\shobs_sched. Avoid your Downloads folder: the shortcuts point at this folder, so it should not move.
  2. Open the folder. It holds files such as sched_app.py, Create shortcuts.bat, Run self-test.bat, run_windows.bat and README.md.
  3. Double-click Create shortcuts.bat. A black window opens. The first time it:
    • says Looking for a Python that can run SHOBS-SCHED... and lists each Python it tries, with usable: beside the one that works, or not usable: and the reason for the others;
    • sets up SHOBS-SCHED's own Python environment in %LOCALAPPDATA%\SHOBS-SCHED\python-env (made from that Python, which is left untouched);
    • says Checking the Python packages SHOBS-SCHED needs (the first time this downloads them: a few minutes)... and installs NumPy, tzdata and Matplotlib;
    • makes a SHOBS-SCHED icon on the Desktop and in the Start menu.
  4. When it says Done. Start SHOBS-SCHED from the Desktop icon or the Start menu., press any key to close the window.

If the window says SHOBS-SCHED could not find a Python it can use, every Python it tried is listed above that line with the reason. Install Python from python.org (tick Add python.exe to PATH) and run Create shortcuts.bat again. If it says The shortcuts could not be made. Leave this window open and copy the message above., the lines above it say why.

Tip: To pin SHOBS-SCHED to the taskbar, open the Start menu, right-click SHOBS-SCHED and choose Pin to taskbar. The pin keeps the SHOBS-SCHED icon (not Python's).

If you ever move the SHOBS-SCHED folder, run Create shortcuts.bat again so the shortcuts point at the new place.

Check the install with the self-test

  1. In the SHOBS-SCHED folder, double-click Run self-test.bat (on Linux or a Mac, see "Linux and the Mac" below).
  2. Wait for it to finish. It says Running the SHOBS-SCHED self-test. It takes under a minute...; on a slow computer the window test can take a little longer. A SHOBS-SCHED window may open and close by itself; that is part of the test.
  3. The last result line should read ALL PASSED.

The test changes none of your settings, targets or schedule: it works in a temporary folder that it deletes at the end. It checks, in seven sections: your Python and its packages (Matplotlib and time zones included); the sky arithmetic (dark times, meridian times, transit times) against a known week of plans; the planner; the weather reader and the exports; the exposure by S/N (new in 1.4.0); the window itself (every page, a build and the ASIAIR list, with the network switched off); and, for information only, whether the online services and the SIMBAD and VizieR mirrors answer.

Each check is marked PASS or FAIL. In the last section a service that does not answer is marked NOTE, not a failure: SHOBS-SCHED falls back on its saved copies. The window section says SKIP when there is no screen.

The log is written to selftest_log.txt in the SHOBS-SCHED folder, and a copy to SHOBS-SCHED_selftest_log.txt in your Downloads folder. If you ever report a problem, attach this file. Chapter 10 has more.

Start SHOBS-SCHED

Double-click the SHOBS-SCHED icon on the Desktop, or choose it from the Start menu. The program opens without a console window, on the Setup page.

The window fills most of the screen. You can resize it or maximize it like any other window.

Other ways to start

run_windows.bat starts SHOBS-SCHED with a black console window beside it, using the same Python as the shortcuts, and checks the packages first. Use it when the program will not start from its icon: the console shows the error, and the window stays open with The app did not start. Leave this window open and copy the message above.

Linux and the Mac

SHOBS-SCHED comes with start files for Linux and the Mac. They do the same job as Create shortcuts.bat on Windows: find a Python that can run SHOBS-SCHED, give SHOBS-SCHED its own Python environment, install the packages, and start the program. They do not make a Desktop icon.

Note: The Linux start file has been tested on Linux. The Mac start files are new and have not yet been tested on a Mac by a user. If one does not work for you, please report it (chapter 10).

Install Python.

  • Mac: install Python 3.13 from python.org/downloads. When it has installed, run Install Certificates.command in its folder under Applications (the lookups need it).
  • Linux: Python 3.11 or newer with Tkinter will do. On Debian or Ubuntu, install Tkinter with sudo apt install python3-tk.

Start SHOBS-SCHED.

  1. Unzip SHOBS-SCHED where you want to keep it.
  2. Start it:
    • Mac: double-click Start SHOBS-SCHED.command. The first time, macOS may refuse to open it. Then Control-click (or right-click) it, choose Open, and Open again.
    • Linux: in a terminal, in the SHOBS-SCHED folder, type bash start_linux.sh.
  3. The first time, it says Setting up SHOBS-SCHED's own Python environment (one time; a few minutes)…, then Trying and the path of each Python it tries. A Python that cannot run SHOBS-SCHED is marked not usable: with the reason. When one works it says ✔ Using and that Python's path, and SHOBS-SCHED opens. Later starts skip all this.

It tries Python 3.13 first, then 3.12, then 3.11, then any newer one. Python 3.13, 3.12 and 3.11 have ready-made copies of NumPy and Matplotlib, and it never compiles a package: a brand-new Python may have none yet. When the packages will not install on one Python, the next one is tried, and a line says why (for example A package has no ready-made copy for this Python and would have to be compiled. Trying an older Python.).

If it ends with ✖ No Python on this computer can run SHOBS-SCHED yet., install Python 3.13 from python.org (on a Mac, then run its Install Certificates.command) and start again. On the Mac, a window that fails stays open with SHOBS-SCHED did not start. Leave this window open and copy the message above. and names the two logs to send with a report: The package install log is ~/Library/Application Support/SHOBS-SCHED/pip_log.txt; the program's own log is ~/.shobs_sched/sched.log.

The Terminal window stays open while SHOBS-SCHED runs. Leave it open until you have closed SHOBS-SCHED.

The self-test. On a Mac, double-click Run self-test.command. On Linux, type bash start_linux.sh selftest. It is the same test as on Windows (above); the log goes to selftest_log.txt in the SHOBS-SCHED folder and a copy to SHOBS-SCHED_selftest_log.txt in your Downloads folder. On the Mac the window says so when it is done. If no Python could be set up, the test never ran and there is no log: the window then says The self-test did not pass or could not run. Leave this window open and copy the message above.

Times, settings files and exports work the same way as on Windows. Buttons that open a file or folder use the system's own opener (xdg-open on Linux, open on the Mac); Open in Notepad opens your default text editor there.

Where SHOBS-SCHED keeps your things

Everything you enter is kept in your user folder (C:\Users\<you> on Windows, your home folder ~ elsewhere), not in the program folder, so an update never loses it:

File or folder What it holds
.shobs_sched_settings.json Setup (site, telescope and camera, mount, limits, horizon, weather, suggestions and darks), the remembered search choices, the ASIAIR export folder and the Include night plots tick.
.shobs_sched_targets.json Your target list: every Campaign, Transit and Variable, with priorities, ticks, goals, rules and notes.
.shobs_sched\schedule.json The saved schedule: every night, its blocks and your Done / Partial / Clouded out marks, plus the history of past nights.
.shobs_sched\cache\ Saved answers from the online services (lookups, the transit catalog, the forecast) and the latest plan card (plan_card.html).
.shobs_sched\sched.log The program's log (chapter 10).

The names start with a dot, so Windows Explorer may hide them. Type %USERPROFILE% into the Explorer address bar and turn on Hidden items under View to see them.

The Python environment is separate again: it holds only Python and its packages. It is in %LOCALAPPDATA%\SHOBS-SCHED on Windows, ~/Library/Application Support/SHOBS-SCHED on a Mac, and ~/.local/share/SHOBS-SCHED on Linux.

Note: Each file is written in one go (to a temporary file first, then swapped in), so a crash never leaves a half-written file. If a file ever cannot be read, SHOBS-SCHED keeps a copy beside it with .bad added to its name and starts from defaults.

Updating to a new version

How you hear of one (new in 1.4.1). At start, SHOBS-SCHED asks GitHub, in the background and at most once a day, whether a newer version is out. When one is, the status strip says so at its right end, in light blue, for example SHOBS-SCHED 1.4.2 is available: Exposure picks name their bound — click for details. Click it: a small New version window lists what the new version brings, with an Open release page button (your web browser opens the release page, with the zip, the users manual and the full notes) and Close. When a release names no highlights, the note says … is available — see the release notes and a click opens the page at once.

SHOBS-SCHED never downloads or installs anything itself, and nothing about you is sent (only the program's name and version, which GitHub asks of every caller). With no internet, or when GitHub does not answer, it says nothing. To turn the check off: Setup → New versions → untick Check for new versions at start. For an email at each release instead: on the GitHub page, Watch → Custom → Releases (or follow https://github.com/SHOBS-S/SHOBS-SCHED/releases.atom in a feed reader).

  1. Unzip the new version, over the old folder or beside it.
  2. On Windows, double-click Create shortcuts.bat in the new folder. It points the shortcuts at it and installs any new packages the new version needs. The Python environment is shared by every SHOBS-SCHED version, so this is quick. On Linux or a Mac, just start the new version with its start file: it checks the packages each time and sets the environment up again if one is missing.

Your settings, targets and schedule stay where they are, in your user folder.

3. Your first night, step by step

This chapter takes you from a fresh install to a printed plan and a target list in ASIAIR. It plans one transit and one variable star. Each step has its own chapter in Part 2 with the details; here we keep to the main road.

The example site is "Example Observatory" at latitude 40.0° N, longitude 100.0° W, with a 0.25 m telescope. Use your own values.

Step 1: Setup

SHOBS-SCHED opens on the Setup page. If SHOBS-P is installed on this computer, many fields are already filled and tagged from SHOBS-P; check them and go on.

  1. In the Site box, type:
    • Name: Example Observatory
    • Latitude: 40.0 (north is positive, south negative)
    • Longitude: -100.0 (east is positive, west is negative)
    • Elevation: your height above sea level.
  2. Check Time zone. SHOBS-SCHED guesses it from the computer's clock and says so under the box (Times show as Local time (UTC−5) now; guessed from this computer's clock: check it and Save.). For our example site pick America/Chicago. You can type in the box to jump through the list.
  3. In Telescope and camera, type the Aperture (250 mm), Focal length and Pixel size. The pixel scale appears beside Binning (for example 1.24″ per pixel). If you have a FITS frame from this camera and telescope, Read from a FITS frame… fills the camera fields for you (chapter 4).
  4. Type your camera's Linear limit in raw ADU, if you know it. It is the brightness above which your camera stops responding in proportion to the light. The planner keeps star peaks under it. If you only have the spec sheet's value, tick Linear limit is from the spec sheet so the plan marks it as an estimate.
  5. In Mount and limits, choose Equatorial (flips) or Alt-az, and check Minimum altitude (30° by default), Darkness to plan in (astronomical by default) and Moon distance at least (By Moon phase by default: targets stay 20° from a crescent Moon up to 70° from a full one; chapter 4).
  6. If you keep a library of darks, type its exposures in Darks you already have, in the Suggestions and darks box (for example 60, 120, 180). The night's Darks to shoot then lists only the others. The other fields in that box filter Find suggested transits… and Find suggested variables…; their starting values will do for now.
  7. Leave the Photometry noise (exposure by S/N) box as it is. Empty, it uses rough estimates; if you have SHOBS-FLUX, its measured profile is found by itself (chapter 4).
  8. Press the green Save button at the bottom. It says ✔ Saved.

Step 2: Add a transit and a variable star

  1. Click 2 Targets in the sidebar.
  2. In Add a target at the top, type WASP-12 b and press Enter (or click Add).
  3. The busy bar appears at the lower left of the window, in the status strip, with the words Looking up WASP-12 b…. When it finishes, a result line appears beside the Add button, for example ✔ Added WASP-12 b (NEA, most precise). The planet is in the Transits box, ticked to plan (☑).
  4. Type DY Peg and press Enter. It is looked up at AAVSO VSX (or its copy at VizieR when VSX does not answer) and lands in the Variables box, also ticked.

Each row shows the target's next event in the planned nights: for a transit, the night and its ingress, mid and egress times; for a variable, its next timed minimum or maximum, or any time for a star with no event worth timing. The Verdict column says whether your telescope can measure it: Detectable, Marginal, Out of reach or Saturates. With Setup's Photometry noise box empty the word ends in "?" (Detectable?): a rough estimate (chapter 5). A transit marked (partial) is only partly in the dark; if a transit shows none in the next 7 nights, no part of any transit is in the dark this week. Pick another planet, or try Find suggested transits… (chapter 5).

Step 3: Build the schedule

  1. Click 3 Schedule in the sidebar.
  2. Check Nights (1 to 7) and first night (tonight by default). These are the same nights the Targets page shows; change them on either page.
  3. Press Build schedule.
  4. The busy bar at the lower left says what it is doing, step by step: first Getting the forecast from Open-Meteo…, then Building 7 nights…, then Working out twilight and Moon times…. When it is done, the busy bar goes, and the line under the button says what was built, for example ✔ Built 7 nights: 9 blocks; 1 target didn't fit (see each night).

Each night gets a tab, named by its date (for example Mon 02 Nov). A ⚠ in front of a tab's name means that night has something that needs your attention, for example a meridian flip during a transit, dew cutting a block, or no dark time. A target that didn't fit, or a cloudy forecast, is listed on the tab but puts no ⚠ on it.

  1. Click the tab of the night you want to observe. You see:
    • a line with the dark hours, the Moon and the weather summary, for example Dark 19:08–05:40 (astronomical) · Moon 35 % (down at dusk, rises 01:12) · Clear all night. The Moon's part follows the dark hours: down at dusk, rises 01:12, up at dusk, sets 22:40, or up all night;
    • the night plot: each target's airmass through the night, its block in bold, twilight shading, the Moon, and the hourly weather above;
    • the nightly table: one row per block, with the time, target, position (RA / Dec), altitude, meridian time, exposure, frames and notes;
    • under it, Flats and darks to shoot, Didn't fit, and why, and Free time.

Click a row (or a block on the plot) to pick it out. Read the notes. Each block's notes start with its verdict, for example Detectable? (S/N ~54, rough estimate): a 1.37 % dip against 1.1 ppt per 10-min bin, 18 bins in transit for WASP-12 b, then its times: Ingress 23:50, mid 01:20, egress 02:50 (±1 min). DY Peg's block starts just before its meridian, so its notes say Start before the meridian and let it flip (~21:01). Pre-flip frames carry no weight in the night mean.

Step 4: Print the plan card

  1. Click 4 OBSPlan in the sidebar. The plan card for the ticked nights is on screen.
  2. Under Nights, tick only the night you will observe (all coming nights are ticked at first).
  3. Leave Include night plots ticked if you want the plot on the card.
  4. Press Print plan card. The card opens in your web browser. Print it from there (in most browsers, Ctrl+P).

The card is black on white with big times, so you can read it at the telescope by red light.

Step 5: Send the night to ASIAIR

  1. On the OBSPlan page, find the night's heading on the card. Beside it are Export to ASIAIR, Export to NINA and Copy ASIAIR list.
  2. Press Export to ASIAIR. A line under the heading says where the file went, for example ✔ ASIAIR: saved 2 targets for Mon 02 Nov to C:\Users\you\Downloads\SHOBS-SCHED_ASIAIR_2026-11-02.csv. In ASIAIR's Plan: Add → Import, and paste the file's text.
  3. Open the file (Open in Notepad), copy its text, and in ASIAIR's Plan, choose Add → Import and paste.

ASIAIR takes the targets' positions, in time order. The times, exposures and notes are on your plan card.

Tip: On the OBSPlan page, Choose folder… can send the ASIAIR files to a folder that syncs to the phone or tablet that runs ASIAIR (iCloud Drive, OneDrive). Then the file is waiting there.

Step 6: The morning after

  1. Open the Schedule page and the night's tab.
  2. Click each row and set its Status: Done, Partial or Clouded out.
  3. When the week moves on, press Re-review schedule. The past nights go into the history, the schedule rolls forward to tonight, and targets that were clouded out or only partly observed go back into the pool to be planned again.

That is the whole cycle. Chapters 4 to 7 cover every page in detail.

4. Setup

The Setup page holds your site and equipment. You fill it in once; after that you come back only when something changes (a new camera, a new horizon survey). Every plan, every time and every exposure estimate depends on it, so it is worth getting right.

New in 1.4.0: the Photometry noise (exposure by S/N) box. It holds what SHOBS-SCHED needs to work out how precisely you can measure a star (the S/N, chapter 5). You can leave it empty: SHOBS-SCHED then uses rough estimates, and says so.

The page subtitle says it plainly: Your site and equipment, entered once. Every time SHOBS-SCHED shows is on the site's clock (its time zone below), whatever this computer's clock says.

This chapter covers:

  • the line at the top (where the values came from) and the source tags,
  • the Site box, including the time zone,
  • the Telescope and camera box, including Read from a FITS frame…,
  • the Mount and limits box (meridian flips, minimum altitude, airmass, darkness, and the default Moon distance),
  • the Horizon profile box,
  • the Weather box (forecast source and dew),
  • the Suggestions and darks box (the filter for the Find buttons, and the darks you already have),
  • the Photometry noise box (a SHOBS-FLUX instrument profile, or your own noise values, for the S/N),
  • saving, and leaving the page with unsaved changes,
  • the planner settings that are not on the page.

The boxes sit in two columns when the window is wide enough: Site, Mount and limits, Weather and Photometry noise (exposure by S/N) on the left; Telescope and camera, Horizon profile, Suggestions and darks and New versions on the right. In a narrow window they sit in one column, in the order this chapter follows. The page scrolls when it does not fit.

Where the values came from

The line at the top of the page says where the values on the page came from:

  • With nothing filled in yet: Enter your site and equipment once (or read the camera from a FITS frame). SHOBS-P, if you add it later, can share these.

Meridian flips

On an equatorial mount, a target crossing the meridian makes the mount flip to the other side of the pier. A flip costs a few minutes (slew, plate solve, guiding restarts), turns the field on the sensor by 180°, and moves the star to other pixels, which is unwelcome in the middle of a transit.

SHOBS-SCHED plans around this. Blocks at a fixed time (transits, eclipse minima) and flexible blocks (campaigns and any-time variables) are placed a little differently.

For every block:

  • It avoids starting a block within an hour after its target's meridian. A start there would make the mount flip at once, while the run is under way. When nothing else fits, the block gets ⚠ Starts 20 min after its meridian: the mount may flip at once (start before 22:40 if you can).
  • A block that ends just past the meridian but inside your Tracks past the meridian margin needs no flip at all. The note says, for example, Ends 3 min past its meridian, inside your tracking margin; stop by 03:38 at the latest.
  • A block that must cross the meridian flips at the start. It starts about 12 minutes before the meridian, so the mount flips right at the start, while you are there, and the rest of the run is on one side of the pier. The note says Start before the meridian and let it flip (~22:40). Pre-flip frames carry no weight in the night mean. for a variable or campaign, and Start before the meridian and let it flip (~22:40). Pre-flip frames are kept; SHOBS-P fits the flip step. for a transit.
  • The flip costs time. The frame count of a block with a flip allows for a few minutes of lost time.

For a fixed-time block, a flip in the middle is noted (Meridian flip at ~01:10; drop the frames during the flip), and a flip during a transit gets a caution: ⚠ Meridian flip during the transit (23:05).

For a flexible block, the planner tries to avoid the flip altogether:

  1. No flip, when a whole block fits before the meridian. If a full-length block fits in the dark and ends no later than your Tracks past the meridian margin after the meridian, the block goes there. There is no flip, no settling and no lost frames.
  2. Otherwise, the flip at the start, as above.
  3. A flip in the middle of the run is the last choice. It puts a pier-side step into a campaign's series, so the planner counts it as costly (about as bad as starting four hours away from the best time) and picks it only when the other places are worse still. Such a block gets ⚠ Meridian flip in the middle of the run (~01:10): a pier-side step in the series.
  4. Never at another target's cost. If running a block wholly before its meridian leaves another target off the night, the planner moves that block back to flip-at-start, as long as everything that fitted still fits and more targets fit. The block's note then says Flips at the start (not run wholly before its meridian) so another target fits tonight.

Set Tracks past the meridian to what your mount really does (in ASIAIR this is its meridian-flip setting). A generous margin lets more blocks finish without a flip. If you clear the field and save, SHOBS-SCHED keeps the value you had saved before (or the 5-minute default); it never turns an empty field into 0, which would make every block flip at once.

On an Alt-az mount there are no flips. Instead, a target that passes higher than 85° gets ⚠ Zenith keyhole with the time: an alt-az mount cannot track smoothly through the zenith.

Southern sites work the same way: flips follow the target's hour angle, wherever the meridian is.

Limits

A target is "up" when it is above Minimum altitude, below Maximum airmass, and above your horizon profile (below). All three apply at once.

Why limits matter for photometry: low stars are seen through more atmosphere. They are fainter, redder and twinkle more, and the extinction changes quickly as they sink. A transit observed from 25° altitude down to 15° has a sloping baseline that is hard to fit. Keep the minimum altitude where your data is still good.

Darkness

Astronomical (Sun −18°) is the default: the planning darkness runs from astronomical dusk to dawn. On short summer nights, or at high latitudes where the Sun never gets 18° below the horizon, choose Nautical (Sun −12°) to plan in what darkness there is. The night tab says when there is no dark time at your chosen level.

Transit baselines may run into nautical twilight even when you plan in astronomical darkness: the transit itself (ingress to egress) must be in the dark, but the out-of-transit stretches can start a little earlier and end a little later.

If SHOBS-P's finder used another darkness (for example Sun −15°), that value appears in the drop-down too.

The Moon distance

A bright Moon near your target brightens the sky around it, and the brighter the Moon, the farther that glow reaches. Moon distance at least keeps every target at least this far from the Moon, so you don't have to set a Moon limit on each target by hand.

  • By Moon phase (the default) picks the distance from how much of the Moon is lit at the middle of the night:

    The Moon's lit fraction Phase Default distance
    under 30 % crescent 20°
    30 % to under 70 % quarter 35°
    70 % to under 95 % gibbous 50°
    95 % or more full 70°

    Waxing and waning count the same. Type your own values in the four By phase boxes.

  • Fixed uses the one distance in Fixed every night, whatever the phase.

  • Off sets no default; only the targets with their own Moon limit have one.

Some details:

  • Only while the Moon is up. Once the Moon has set (or before it rises), the distance does not matter.
  • A target's own limit wins. A target with its own Moon at least in its Edit dialog (chapter 5) uses that, even when it is smaller than the default. Leave a target's field empty to use this default.
  • It applies to every block, fixed-time ones (transits, timed minima) as well as flexible ones.
  • Find suggested transits… skips a transit if the Moon comes closer than this at any time during it, while the Moon is up (chapter 5).
  • When the Moon spoils a target, the reason says which limit was used, for example Moon too close (38° at best; default for a gibbous Moon 50°), Moon too close (38° at best; Setup's default 30°) with Fixed, or Moon too close (38° at best; your limit 40°) for a target's own limit (chapter 6).
  • The limits: each value is 0 to 180 degrees. An empty box goes back to its default when you save. A wrong value gives, for example, Moon distance for a gibbous Moon must be between 0 and 180 degrees.

How this differs from Moon-brightened sky up to (in Suggestions and darks, below): that one is about how much the Moon brightens the sky over the whole transit, and is used only by the two Finds. Moon distance at least is a plain distance, used by the planner for every block and by Find suggested transits….

Horizon profile

If trees or buildings block part of your sky, give SHOBS-SCHED your horizon:

  • Type one azimuth altitude pair per line, in degrees, for example 120 25 (at azimuth 120°, the horizon is at 25° altitude). Commas or semicolons between the numbers work too, and anything after a # on a line is ignored.
  • Or press Import CSV… and choose a CSV or text file with azimuth and altitude columns. Lines that are not two numbers are skipped. The message says how many points were read; press Save to keep them.
  • Leave the box empty for a flat horizon. The minimum altitude always applies on top.

Azimuths run from 0 to 360 (north 0, east 90); altitudes from −5 to 90. Under Azimuths measured from, choose true north, or magnetic north if your survey was made with a compass; then type your Declination (the magnetic declination, east positive) so the profile is turned to true north.

Photometry noise (exposure by S/N)

New in 1.4.0. SHOBS-SCHED can now work out, for each target, how precisely your telescope and camera can measure it, and whether its transit or its light changes will show: the S/N and a verdict word (Detectable, Marginal, Out of reach or Saturates; chapter 5). For that it needs a few facts about your system that the other boxes do not hold, such as how many electrons a star of a given magnitude gives each second. This box is where they come from.

You do not have to fill it in. With the box empty, SHOBS-SCHED uses rough estimates for everything, the same model 1.3.0 used for its exposures. You still get an S/N and a verdict, but the verdict word then carries a ? (Detectable?, Marginal?, Out of reach?, Saturates?) to say it is a guess, not a measurement.

Where the numbers come from

Each number is taken from the first of these that has it:

  1. A SHOBS-FLUX instrument profile (tag from FLUX). SHOBS-FLUX is the SHOBS program that measures your telescope and camera, on the bench and from your nights under the sky. It keeps what it measured in profiles in the .shobs folder in your user folder. SHOBS-SCHED only reads them: it never writes them, and it works fully without them. A value from the profile is shown grayed, and you cannot type over it: what was measured wins.
  2. What you type in this box (tag set here).
  3. A rough estimate (tag estimate, with the value used, for example estimate 21.5). This is either the profile's own first guess, or SHOBS-SCHED's.

Instrument profile

The Instrument profile drop-down chooses the SHOBS-FLUX profile to read:

  • Automatic (matches the telescope and camera), the default: the profile whose camera is the one in Setup's Camera field (at your Gain setting and Binning, when the profile names them), whose telescope has the name in Setup's Telescope field (when you typed one), and whose focal length is within 3 % of Setup's. (ASIAIR's plate-solved focal length moves a few millimeters from night to night, so the match is not exact.) When several match, the nearest focal length wins.
  • None (use the values below): no profile; your typed values, or rough estimates.

Saving

Press the green Save button at the bottom. It says ✔ Saved., and the status strip says ✔ Setup saved.

If a value is wrong, nothing is saved and the line says why, for example ✖ Not saved: Longitude must be between −180 and 180 (degrees, east positive, west negative). When several values are wrong, it shows the first and says how many more there are.

Leaving the page with unsaved changes. If you go to another page (or close the window) with valid changes, they are saved for you, and the status strip says ✔ Setup saved: you left the page with changes, so they were kept. If the changes cannot be saved, SHOBS-SCHED asks whether to drop them.

Changing Setup after a schedule was built. The schedule was worked out with the old values. The Schedule and OBSPlan pages then show a ⚠ line asking you to build again (chapter 6).

5. Targets

The Targets page is your list of what you want to measure. You add targets by name, tick the ones to plan, and let SHOBS-SCHED suggest more for the coming nights.

Its subtitle reads: What you want to measure. Tick Plan (☑) to plan a target, click its Priority to change it, select a row for its details, and double-click it to edit. Each list shows the next event in the planned nights; drag the bar between Transits and Variables to share the room.

This chapter covers:

  • the three boxes (Campaigns, Transits, Variables) and their columns, including the Verdict,
  • the Nights row,
  • adding a target by name, and what happens with stars that are not a planet or a known variable,
  • Plan, Priority, Keep, Edit… and Remove,
  • the Edit dialog, including the Exposure section (Best precision, Longest safe or your own exposure, the S/N and its verdict), a star's known planets and no-transit windows,
  • how SHOBS-SCHED works out the S/N, with a worked example,
  • Find suggested transits… and Find suggested variables…,
  • ephemeris precision, and how SHOBS-SCHED upgrades it for you.

The three boxes

Targets sit in three boxes, by type. Each box has its own scroll bars, and the mouse wheel scrolls the box under the pointer.

Box What goes in it How it is planned
Campaigns Stars or fields you visit on many nights: a long-running variable star program, a star field you monitor, a planet host you watch between transits. A flexible block at the best part of the night, with an optional weekly goal and its own rules.
Transits Transiting exoplanets with an ephemeris. At the transit's time, with baseline before and after.
Variables Variable stars from AAVSO VSX. Eclipsing binaries at their timed minima; pulsators and long-period stars as flexible blocks at any time of night.

Campaigns is a short box on top. Transits and Variables share the rest of the page; drag the bar between them to give one more room.

Each box's title says how many targets it holds and how many are ticked to plan, and which clock the times are on, for example Transits (9, 2 planned) · next event in the 7 planned nights, Local time (UTC−5 → UTC−6) (the arrow appears when the clocks change during the planned nights). An empty box shows a hint, such as none yet: Add a planet, or press Find suggested transits.

The columns

Every box starts with Plan (☑ or ☐), Name and V (the star's V magnitude; for a variable, its brightest). The others depend on the box:

Box Columns
Campaigns Type, Period, Next useful event, This week, Verdict, Priority, RA / Dec (J2000), Source

A campaign's Type and Period are the star's VSX type and period (new in 1.4.0). They are kept when you move a variable star to Campaigns. A campaign that is not a known variable shows — in both.

The Verdict column

New in 1.4.0. Verdict says whether your telescope and camera can measure the target, in one word:

Verdict Means
Detectable (green) S/N 10 or more: the transit, or the star's light changes, will show clearly.
Marginal (yellow) S/N 5 to 10: it will show, but only just. A good night helps.
Out of reach S/N under 5: lost in the noise with your telescope.
Saturates The star's predicted peak is over your linear limit at this exposure, whatever the S/N. Shorten the exposure or defocus.
— Nothing to judge: the target has no V magnitude, or a variable or campaign has no VSX range.

A word with a ? (Detectable?, Marginal?, Out of reach?, Saturates?) is a rough estimate: there is no measured zero point for your system yet (Setup's Photometry noise box, chapter 4), so the word is a guess. It uses the same S/N limits.

The verdict is worked out with the target's exposure, at the target's best airmass from your site, under a dark sky (no Moon). A suggested transit's verdict is for its own night instead (that night's airmass and Moon). The Schedule page works it out again for each night (chapter 6). How the S/N is worked out is explained under "How SHOBS-SCHED works out the S/N" below.

The next event in the planned nights

The event columns show each target's first useful event in the planned nights (the Nights row, below), on the site's clock. They are worked out in the background after the page opens; until then they show ….

  • Transits: the night, ingress, mid-transit and egress of the first transit that happens entirely in the dark with the star above your limits. If only part of a transit is visible, the night says (partial). If none falls in the planned nights: none in the next 7 nights (or none tonight for one night).
  • Variables: the night and the time of the next timed event, for example min 01:14 (an eclipsing binary's minimum) or max 22:05. A star with no timed event shows any time on the first night it is up long enough. A suggested star whose published elements are too old to give a good time shows any time ⚠ time uncertain ±45 min (or ⚠ no usable timing); a short pulsator suggested for a whole cycle shows any time (a whole cycle).
  • Campaigns: every night, or, for a campaign with a no-transit window, every night; next blackout Wed 04 Nov 21:43–23:22 (HD 219134 c transits). The blackout is looked for from dusk on the first planned night to the end of the last one, so with Nights from Mon 02 Nov it never names an earlier day.

Days are written the same way everywhere in SHOBS-SCHED, on the Targets page and in the plan: weekday, day, month, as in Wed 04 Nov.

The rows are sorted by these events: Transits soonest transit first; Variables night by night, with each night's timed minima and maxima first, then its any-time stars; Campaigns by name. Rows with no event in the planned nights come last. A row stays in its place when you tick or untick it.

The Source column

Source says where the target came from: ✔ NEA or ✔ NEA (most precise) (the NASA Exoplanet Archive; the second when a more precise published ephemeris than the Archive's default was chosen), ✔ VSX or ✔ VizieR VSX (AAVSO VSX, directly or through VizieR's copy), ✔ SIMBAD, ✔ NASA Exoplanet Archive (a star whose position came from the Archive, see "Add a target by name"), or typed. A suggestion shows suggested · NEA or suggested · VizieR VSX until you tick it or press Keep; then it shows ✔ NEA or ✔ VizieR VSX. A transit found with a Priority list (see "Find suggested transits") adds its priority, for example suggested · NEA · ExoClock high, SG1 2; a TESS candidate that is not yet in the Archive shows suggested · ExoFOP · SG1 1 (or · ExoClock for a planet only ExoClock lists). A transit whose predicted mid-time is uncertain by more than 10 minutes adds a ⚠ Tc ± note (see "Ephemeris precision" below).

Details of the selected target

Click a row to see its details on a line under the boxes: for a transit, its depth, duration, period, the predicted mid-time uncertainty (Tc ± 1.4 min) and where the ephemeris came from; for a variable, its type and period; for a campaign, its rules, goal and progress; and for every target, how its exposure is chosen (exposure: Best precision (by S/N), exposure: Longest safe, or exposure 60 s (yours)), its verdict with the reason, its block length and notes. A suggestion also shows its score.

The verdict part reads, for example, Detectable? (at its best airmass, dark sky): S/N ~54 (rough estimate, no measured zero point): a 1.37 % dip against 1.1 ppt per 10-min bin, 18 bins in transit. A suggestion's says (on its night) instead.

The Nights row

Under Add a target is the Nights row: from (tonight, or any of the next 13 nights) for (1 to 7 nights; 1, tonight only, at first). The hint beside it says these nights are searched by Find, shown in the lists below, and built on the Schedule page. These are the nights that:

  • the event columns look at,
  • Find suggested transits… and Find suggested variables… search,
  • the Schedule page builds.

The Schedule page has the same two controls; changing them on either page changes both. The number of nights is remembered between sessions; the first night goes back to tonight each time you start the program.

Add a target by name

  1. Type a name in Add a target: a planet (WASP-12 b, TrES-3b, toi 2046 b), a variable star (DY Peg, V0404 Lyr) or any star SIMBAD (or the NASA Exoplanet Archive) knows.
  2. Press Enter or click Add.
  3. The busy bar at the lower left says Looking up DY Peg…. When it is done, a result line beside Add says ✔ Added DY Peg (VSX). For a planet it names the source and the ephemeris in one bracket, for example ✔ Added WASP-12 b (NEA, most precise).

The new target is ticked to plan, selected, and its details are shown.

How the name is looked up:

  1. A planet name (or a star the NASA Exoplanet Archive knows as a planet host) is looked up at the NASA Exoplanet Archive. You get the planet's position, V, depth, duration and the most precise published ephemeris. Spellings are forgiving: wasp-12b, WASP 12 b and WASP-12 b all work.
  2. Otherwise, a variable star is looked up at AAVSO VSX, or VizieR's copy of it when VSX does not answer. You get its type, period, epoch and range. Eclipsing binaries are planned at their minima (each minimum covered ±60 minutes, or ±0.6 × the eclipse length when VSX gives it, between ±20 and ±240 minutes); pulsating and long-period stars are planned at any time of night.
  3. Otherwise, the star's position is looked up at SIMBAD, and a window asks what the star is for you (below). SHOBS-SCHED asks SIMBAD at the CDS (Strasbourg) first, then its mirror at IDIA (Cape Town) when the CDS does not answer. When SIMBAD does not answer at all, or does not know the name, a planet host's position is taken from the NASA Exoplanet Archive instead. This also works with a planet's name: V1298 Tau d gives its star, V1298 Tau. A star added this way shows ✔ NASA Exoplanet Archive as its Source, and the result line says where the position came from with a ⚠, for example ⚠ Added V1298 Tau (NASA Exoplanet Archive). Position from the NASA Exoplanet Archive (SIMBAD did not answer). When the position is already known and only the star's planets are still to be looked up, the busy bar says Looking up V1298 Tau's known planets… instead of Looking up the star V1298 Tau….

Some results need a word from you:

  • A star with several planets. Typing a host star (for example HD 219134) asks "HD 219134 has more than one planet. Add one of them as a transit target, or the star itself (for example a campaign that keeps clear of the planets' transits)?" Pick a planet, or The star itself (a campaign or a variable). Planets that do not transit are left out of the choice and named in the question.
  • A star that is not a planet or a known variable. A window asks What kind of target?, with the choices Campaign (a star you visit on many nights), Variable star and Star field (a campaign), plus Transit target (type its ephemeris later) when the star has no known planets. If the star has transiting planets, they are offered too, and a campaign or variable made from the star gets "no data while … transits" for each of them straight away.
  • A planet that does not transit is never added as an empty transit target. SHOBS-SCHED offers its star instead.
  • A name already in the list gives ⚠ DY Peg is already in the list.
  • A name nobody knows. When SIMBAD answers that it does not know the name (and no other service knows it either), the result line says ✖ No planet, variable star or SIMBAD object is called Foo 123. Check the spelling. When a star's position is looked up and neither SIMBAD nor the Archive knows the name, it says ✖ Could not find Foo 123's position: Neither SIMBAD nor the NASA Exoplanet Archive knows Foo 123. Check the spelling, or type the coordinates by hand. SHOBS-SCHED says this only when SIMBAD's reply really says the name is unknown. Any other error from SIMBAD counts as SIMBAD not answering (next point), so a problem on SIMBAD's side is never reported as a wrong name.
  • A service that does not answer. If the name was looked up before, the saved copy is used, and the result line says so with a ⚠: ⚠ Added DY Peg (VSX). AAVSO VSX did not answer (no answer within 10 s; VizieR's copy: service unavailable, HTTP 503); using the copy from 3 days ago. (VSX is given 10 seconds, then VizieR's copy is tried; the saved copy is used only when both fail, so both reasons are named.) If not, it says so in plain words, for example ✖ The NASA Exoplanet Archive did not answer (service unavailable, HTTP 503) and there is no saved copy for WASP-12 b. Try again later. SIMBAD is the same: when neither SIMBAD (at the CDS or its mirror) nor the Archive can give the star's position, the line says, for example, ✖ SIMBAD did not answer (SIMBAD reported a problem on its side; its mirror: no answer within 30 s) and there is no saved copy for HD 12345. Try again later. When the Archive or VSX did not answer but SIMBAD knows the star, nothing is added: the line names the service that did not answer and ends with what SIMBAD calls the star ((SIMBAD knows … as ….), or (the NASA Exoplanet Archive has … as ….) when the position came from the Archive), since the name may still be a planet or a variable. Try again later.

Tip: A campaign that orbits a planet host, for example a star you monitor for flares, should have no data while its planets transit, or your light curve will have dips that are not the star's. SHOBS-SCHED offers these windows when you add the star; you can change them later in Edit….

Plan, Priority, Keep and Remove

It stays in the list even if you untick it later.

  • Priority. Click a row's Priority cell and choose High, Normal or Low. Priority decides who wins when two targets want the same time: flexible targets are placed High first, then Normal, then Low; and between two fixed-time targets that collide, the higher priority wins.

  • Keep (the button Keep (a suggestion stays when you Find again)). A search replaces every suggestion of its kind that is still a suggestion. Select one or more suggestions and press Keep to make them your own without ticking them: they stay in the list, and Source shows ✔ NEA or ✔ VizieR VSX. The line says, for example, ✔ Kept DY Peg: a new Find no longer replaces it. (Keep on a target that is not a suggestion says ⚠ Select a suggested transit or variable to keep.)

  • Remove removes the selected targets. A window titled Remove targets asks Remove DY Peg from the list? Blocks already planned stay until you rebuild.; press Remove to go ahead, or Cancel. The line then says ✔ Removed DY Peg.

  • Edit… (or double-click a row) opens the Edit dialog for one target.

Blocks you locked or set by hand

It asks:

  • when you untick a target that has such blocks (from tonight on),
  • when you Remove such a target (the middle sentence then reads A rebuild keeps blocks set by hand even when the target is gone.),
  • when you press Build schedule or Re-review on the Schedule page and such blocks belong to targets that are unticked or no longer in the list. (Their targets are … for several blocks), Its target is no longer in your list., or, for a mix, Their targets are not ticked to plan, or no longer in your list.

Exposure

New in 1.4.0 (it replaces the single Exposure field of 1.3.0's Observing part). It sets how this target's exposure is chosen, and shows what that exposure buys you: the precision, the S/N and the verdict.

How the exposure is chosen. Three buttons:

  • Best precision: the exposure that gives the best light curve. Of the standard exposures (0.75 s to 300 s) whose predicted peak stays under your linear-limit aim (80 % of the linear limit), SHOBS-SCHED takes the shortest one that is within 2 % of the best precision per 10-minute bin. Longer frames would gain almost nothing, and shorter frames time ingress and egress better.
  • Longest safe: the longest standard exposure whose predicted peak stays under the aim. With no SHOBS-FLUX profile and nothing typed in Setup's Photometry noise box it chooses as 1.3.0 did (the peak it shows is the noise model's); otherwise the peak is the noise model's (the same one the verdict Saturates uses), so Longest safe never picks an exposure the verdict calls Saturates. The warnings on My exposure follow the same rule.
  • My exposure: your own, in seconds, in the box beside it. Typing in the box chooses My exposure for you.

The gray line under the buttons says the same in short: Best precision: the shortest exposure within 2 % of the best 10-minute precision whose peak stays under your linear-limit aim (the default for transits once a zero point is measured by SHOBS-FLUX or typed in Setup). Longest safe: the longest standard exposure whose predicted peak stays under the aim (1.3.0's rule while no SHOBS-FLUX profile or typed value is in use). My exposure: yours.

The three bounds (new in 1.4.1). Best precision and Longest safe both keep inside three bounds, and the readout names the one that decided the exposure:

  1. Saturation. Not only the target: the stars you will measure must stay under the aim too. When the target has a SHOBS-P series (the SHOBS-P series field below), these are its comparison and check stars. Without one, they are the likely comps: the APASS stars in the field from the target's brightness up to 1.5 mag brighter (from the comp check, which Build schedule also runs for you). A brighter field star nobody would use as a comp only gets a plain note when it would be over the linear limit.
  2. Cadence. Frames must come often enough to time the event: the frame cycle (exposure plus download) at most P/50 for a variable or campaign with a period, and at most the ingress ÷ 10 for a transit. The ingress comes from the planet's duration, impact parameter and size; when the Archive does not give those, SCHED takes it as 10 % of the duration and says so.
  3. Precision. Inside those two, Best precision picks as described above; Longest safe takes the longest.

The Bounds line under the Exposure line shows all three at once, for example Bounds: 180 s, cadence-limited (P/50 = 4.3 min) · peak 61 % of linear ✓ (check star: TYC 3281-582-1) · 1.2 ppt / 10 min, Detectable · cap costs 1 % vs 300 s. "Cap costs" is how much precision the cap gives up against the exposure SCHED would take without it; when that is a lot, or the verdict drops, a note says so: you can still trade timing for photons with My exposure.

The default. When you have not chosen, a target gets:

  • a transit: Best precision, but only when the zero point is known: measured by a SHOBS-FLUX profile or typed in Setup's Photometry noise box. Until then it gets Longest safe, and the gray line at the bottom of the readout ends Longest safe until a zero point is measured or typed in Setup (then Best precision is the default; your own choice above always wins). The reason: Best precision trusts the noise model, and with only rough numbers it should not pick your exposures.
  • a variable or campaign: Longest safe.

Your own choice always wins. Click a button and Save, and the target keeps that way whatever Setup says later — even a click on the button that is already chosen pins it (so you can keep a transit on Longest safe for good). If you do not touch the buttons, the target keeps following the default (change its Group and the buttons show the new group's default at once): a transit on Longest safe today moves to Best precision by itself once its zero point is measured or typed.

Band. The band the S/N is worked out for. The drop-down lists only bands SHOBS-SCHED has a zero point for: the bands your SHOBS-FLUX profile has measured, or else Setup's main band, Green channel (TG) on a color camera or Clear band (CV) on a mono one. Beside it, a tag says where the numbers came from: from FLUX, set here, estimate (no FLUX profile) or estimate (FLUX has no measured zero point yet).

Under it, a gray line says which magnitude was used. SHOBS-SCHED knows only the star's V magnitude:

  • For the green channel, clear, or a V filter it takes V as it is: V 11.57 → your green channel (TG) (taken as V).
  • For another band (red or blue channel, B, R, I, the Sloan bands) it uses the color of a Sun-like star: V 11.57 → your red channel (TR) (R ≈ V − 0.39 for a Sun-like star; SCHED knows only V, a redder or bluer star differs).

How SHOBS-SCHED works out the S/N

The S/N ("signal to noise") says how clearly the thing you want to see stands out from the noise of your measurement. SHOBS-SCHED works it out the same way for the Verdict column, the Edit dialog, Find suggested transits, the Schedule and the plan card. You do not need any of this to use the program, but it helps to know what the verdict rests on.

What goes in

Input Where it comes from
The star's V magnitude The target (Edit → Target). For another band, a Sun-like star's color (see "Band" above).
The transit's depth and duration, or the variable's range The ephemeris (Edit → Ephemeris), or the star's VSX range.
Aperture and pixel scale Setup's Telescope and camera box.
Zero point, extinction, dark sky, seeing, read noise, dark current, scintillation C_Y from FLUX (a SHOBS-FLUX profile), else set here (Setup's Photometry noise box), else an estimate (chapter 4).
Gain (e⁻ per ADU), linear limit, per-frame overhead The SHOBS-FLUX profile, else Setup's Telescope and camera box. A target's own Seconds between frames wins for the overhead.
Site elevation Setup's Site box (higher sites twinkle less).
Airmass and the Moon The target's best airmass under a dark sky (Targets, Edit), the suggestion's night (Find), or the block's own airmass and Moon (Schedule). The Moon's light is added to your sky.

The steps

  1. How many electrons the star gives. From the zero point, the star's magnitude and the airmass (the extinction dims it a little more at each extra airmass).
  2. The noise of one frame. Four parts:
    • photon noise: the star's own light arrives at random; the more electrons, the smaller this is, relatively;
    • sky: the sky's light inside the photometry aperture (1.5 × the seeing, in radius), more with the Moon up;
    • scintillation: the twinkling of the air, larger for small telescopes, short frames and high airmass;
    • read + dark: the camera's read noise and dark current in the aperture's pixels.
  3. The precision of a 10-minute bin. All the frames of 10 minutes are averaged (the exposure plus the per-frame overhead sets how many). Averaging n frames divides the noise by √n.
  4. The S/N. For a transit: the depth, divided by the noise of the whole transit, which is the 10-minute precision divided by √(number of 10-minute bins in the transit). For a variable: its VSX range (as a fraction of its light) divided by the precision of one 10-minute bin.
  5. The verdict. S/N 10 or more is Detectable, 5 to 10 Marginal, under 5 Out of reach. If the predicted peak is over the linear limit, the verdict is Saturates, whatever the S/N. With an estimated zero point, the word gets a ?.

A worked example

WASP-12 b from the example site (latitude 40° N, a 250 mm telescope with a color camera at 0.48″ per pixel, 1 e⁻ per ADU, linear limit 32,000 ADU), with Setup's Photometry noise box empty and no SHOBS-FLUX profile:

  • WASP-12 is V 11.57. Take its transit as 1.47 % deep and 3.0 hours long (the Archive's values may differ a little). It passes at 79.7° altitude here, airmass 1.02.
  • The zero point is the rough estimate, 19.76 for the green channel. A V 11.57 star then gives about 1,900 electrons a second.
  • The transit has no SHOBS-FLUX measurement, so it is on Longest safe: 180 s, with a predicted peak of about 15,500 ADU, 48 % of the linear limit.
  • In 180 s the aperture collects about 340,000 electrons. One frame's noise is about 2.0 ppt (parts per thousand): photon noise 77 % of it, scintillation 22 %, the sky 1 %.
  • With 3 s of overhead, a 10-minute bin holds about 3.3 frames, so its precision is 2.0 ÷ √3.3 ≈ 1.1 ppt.
  • The transit lasts 3.0 hours, which is 18 ten-minute bins, so the noise of the whole transit is 1.1 ÷ √18 ≈ 0.25 ppt.
  • The depth is 1.47 % = 14.7 ppt. The S/N is 14.7 ÷ 0.25 ≈ 58: well over 10, so Detectable. The zero point was only an estimate, so the verdict reads Detectable?, and the readout shows S/N 58 Detectable?.

A planet with a 0.5 % transit, 2 hours long, on a V 13.0 star gives S/N ~9 with the same telescope: Marginal?.

What the S/N leaves out

The S/N is optimistic, on purpose kept simple:

  • The target star alone. The comparison stars add noise of their own (a little, with bright comps; more with faint ones).
  • No systematics. Real light curves have more than random noise: changing transparency, the star drifting on the sensor, flat-field errors, the meridian flip, color trends as the star sinks. None of that is in the S/N, and at very high S/N it is what limits you. A light curve better than about 1 ppt per 10 minutes is rare from the ground.
  • A rough estimate is rough. Without a measured zero point, the S/N can be off by a factor of two either way.

So read Marginal as "worth a try on a good night", and Detectable? as "probably, but not yet measured". Once the zero point is measured by SHOBS-FLUX or typed in Setup, the verdicts lose their "?" and transits move to Best precision (unless you pinned another way).

Ephemeris precision

A transit's predicted mid-time is only as good as its ephemeris. An ephemeris published years ago, with a small error in the period, can be off by an hour today. That is why SHOBS-SCHED cares where each ephemeris came from:

  • Adding a planet looks at every published ephemeris for it at the Archive and keeps the one with the smallest predicted uncertainty for the coming weeks (it keeps the Archive's default set unless another beats it by more than 5 %).
  • Suggestions first come from the Archive's planet list, then the shortlist is checked again with each planet's most precise ephemeris, so a stale one neither offers a transit that is not there nor hides one that is.
  • Upgrading. A transit still carrying the planet list's ephemeris (for example a suggestion you ticked or kept) is upgraded in the background to the most precise one: when you tick it, when you keep it, and each time SHOBS-SCHED starts. The busy bar says Checking transit ephemerides at the NASA Exoplanet Archive…, and when one changes the line under the boxes says ✔ Most precise ephemeris now used for TOI-1234 b (was the Archive's planet-list value). Build the schedule to apply.

The predicted mid-time uncertainty (Tc ± 1.4 min) is shown in the target's details. Above 10 minutes it gets a ⚠ in the Source column and in the plan's notes (⚠ Ephemeris uncertain: mid-time ± 25 min). Such a transit is still worth observing (a new timing is valuable), but plan extra baseline: the transit may come early or late.

Before it builds, SHOBS-SCHED checks for blocks you locked or set by hand (and have not marked yet), from the first night on, whose target is no longer ticked or no longer in your list. If there are any, a window titled Blocks set by hand asks first, for example: BL Cam has 2 blocks you locked or set by hand on Tue 03 Nov and Wed 04 Nov. Their targets are not ticked to plan. Remove them too? (Cancel keeps them in the schedule, with a note that the target is not planned.) The middle sentence says … no longer in your list. for removed targets, and Their targets are not ticked to plan, or no longer in your list. for a mix. Remove takes them out; Cancel keeps them, and each kept block gets the note ⚠ This target is not ticked to plan (or ⚠ This target is no longer in your list). Building again keeps your work: every block you locked, set the times of, or marked (Done, Partial, Clouded out) stays on its night with its times and its mark, and the rest of the night is rebuilt around it. A build that starts later than the saved schedule moves the nights before it into the history, marks included.

Tip: Building needs your site (latitude, longitude, time zone) and at least one ticked target. Without a site the page says ✖ Enter your site on the Setup page first (latitude, longitude, time zone); then build. Without a ticked target it says ⚠ No targets are ticked to plan (☑ on the Targets page).

How a night is filled

The planner fills each night in a fixed order. Knowing it helps you read the plan:

  1. Your blocks first. Locked blocks, blocks whose times you set, and blocks already marked stay exactly as they are. If one is no longer possible (the target is below your limits, outside the darkness, inside a no-transit window, or overlapping another of your blocks), it stays and gets a ⚠. One whose target you unticked or removed stays too, with an information note (see the Build question above).
  2. Fixed-time targets next. Transits (from the baseline before ingress to the baseline after egress) and timed variable events (a minimum or maximum ± its half-width). The transit itself must be in the planning darkness and above your limits, and, while the Moon is up, no closer to it than the target's Moon at least or Setup's default Moon distance at least (chapter 4); the baselines may run into nautical twilight, and may be shortened to fit the night. When two fixed-time targets collide, their baselines are trimmed first, down to 20 minutes a side (⚠ Baseline trimmed). If they still collide, one wins: higher priority first, then the rarer chance (a transit not seen again for a month beats one that comes back in three nights), then the better signal-to-noise.
  3. Flexible targets last. Campaigns and any-time variables, High priority first, then Normal, then Low. A campaign behind its weekly goal moves up one level. Each gets at most one block a night, of its Block length (or less when there is less room, but never under its Shortest useful block), placed at the best part of the night for it: on an equatorial mount wholly before its meridian when a full block fits there, otherwise starting about 12 minutes before its meridian (see Flexible blocks and the meridian flip, below); on an alt-az mount centred on the meridian. Its own rules apply: Own minimum altitude, Moon at least (or, when that is empty, Setup's default Moon distance at least, by the Moon's phase that night), Only between, and its no-transit windows.
  4. Overheads. A few minutes between blocks for slewing and plate solving, autofocus at the start of the night, and a refocus after a defocused target.
  5. Weather. With the dew setting on, flexible blocks end when dew is expected (Setup, chapter 4).

Flexible blocks and the meridian flip

This applies to a German equatorial mount (Mount in Setup). An alt-az mount never flips.

When a target crosses the meridian, an equatorial mount must turn to the other side of the pier. The star then lands on a different part of the sensor, the field is turned 180°, and the guiding and plate solve start again. For a campaign or an any-time variable you want one long, even series, so a flip in the middle of a run is the worst place for it: it leaves a step in the light curve. The planner places flexible blocks so that this does not happen:

  1. No flip, if a full block fits. When a whole block of the target's Block length fits in the dark before the meridian, the block runs wholly before it, ending inside your Tracks past the meridian margin (Setup). There is no flip at all, and no frames to drop. The note says, for example, Ends 5 min past its meridian, inside your tracking margin; stop by 23:47 at the latest: stop the run by then, or the mount flips.
  2. Otherwise, flip at the start. When a full block does not fit before the meridian, the block starts about 12 minutes before the meridian. The mount flips while you are there at the start, and the rest of the run is on one side of the pier. The note says Start before the meridian and let it flip (~22:40). Pre-flip frames carry no weight in the night mean. (A transit's note differs; see "Notes you will see" below.)
  3. A flip in the middle only as a last resort. The planner counts a mid-run flip as a heavy cost (as bad as starting 4 hours from the best time), so it only places one there when nothing better fits. The block then gets ⚠ Meridian flip in the middle of the run (~01:10): a pier-side step in the series.

No flip, but not at another target's cost. Running a block wholly before the meridian puts it earlier in the night than a flip-at-start block. Sometimes that takes the room another target needed. When targets didn't fit, the planner tries each no-flip block at flip-at-start instead, and keeps the change only when every target that fitted still fits and at least one more does. Every block moved this way then says Flips at the start (not run wholly before its meridian) so another target fits tonight. Blocks you locked or set the times of are never changed this way. Only a room that holds the whole block counts as "fits before the meridian": a shorter no-flip run never wins over a full flip-at-start block.

Fixed-time blocks (transits, timed events) cannot move: their flip falls where it falls, with a note (and a ⚠ when it is inside the transit).

The night tabs

Each night has a tab named by its date, for example Tue 20 Oct. A ⚠ in front of the name means the night needs your attention: a block still planned (not yet marked) has a caution you should act on, or the night has a night-wide ✖ (no dark time). A target that didn't fit, a cloudy forecast, a night-wide ⚠ line above the plot (such as ⚠ Dew expected from 04:05; a block that the dew reaches still has its own caution), and the cautions ⚠ Baseline trimmed, ⚠ Ephemeris uncertain, ⚠ This target is no longer in your list and ⚠ This target is not ticked to plan do not put a ⚠ on the tab; you still see them on the night itself. A night that has passed says (past).

A tab is built the first time you open it. At the top is the night's line: the date, the dark hours and which darkness (Dark 19:42–05:58 (astronomical)), the Moon (below) and the weather summary (Clear until 02:00, then 60–80 % cloud; dew from 04:05). Under it come any night-wide warnings, such as ⚠ Dew expected from 04:05, or ✖ No dark time tonight: the Sun gets no lower than −15.2° (planning darkness −18.0°); nautical darkness (Sun below −12°) is possible if you allow it in Setup.

The Moon is given in the order the dark night meets it: whether it is up or down at dusk, then what it does before dawn. For example Moon 62 % (down at dusk, rises 23:10), Moon 48 % (up at dusk, sets 01:35), or, when it does not rise or set in the dark, Moon 95 % (up all night) or Moon 3 % (down all night).

The night plot

The night plot shows the whole night at a glance. Its design follows the airmass plot of TAPIR by Eric Jensen (chapter 9).

  • Across: the time on the site's clock, from 1.5 hours before nautical dusk to 1.5 hours after nautical dawn, with the sunset, twilight and sunrise times along the top. The sky is shaded by twilight: lighter at dusk and dawn, darkest in astronomical night.
  • Down: airmass on the left (1.0 at the top), elevation (altitude) on the right. The red dashed line is your maximum airmass (max airmass 2).
  • Title: the date, your site's name and the Moon's phase; at the right, the selected block (Selected: HAT-P-6 b (Transit) 19:05–00:36).
  • Each planned target: its path through the whole night is drawn faint; its block is drawn bold in its type's color (green Transit, navy Variable, plum Campaign), with the airmass at the block's start and end. A label box gives its name, start and end, and its distance from the Moon.
  • Transits: the transit itself (ingress to egress) is a green band with its times (Transit 20:05–23:36), its baselines a fainter green band.
  • Meridian: a ▼ marks a meridian flip inside a block (flip 21:27); a | marks a meridian crossing that needs no flip (meridian 21:05).
  • The Moon: a dashed gold line, thicker when fuller.
  • No-transit windows: hatched, but only where one changed the plan.
  • Locked blocks and blocks you set the times of have a gold box edge.
  • Weather strip (above the plot, on the same time axis): gray bars for cloud %, a red line for temperature and a blue line for the dew point, the spread (temperature minus dew point) as numbers along the top, a blue fill where dew is likely (spread under your Setup threshold), and a dashed line with ⚠ dew from 04:05 at the dew onset. With no forecast, the strip says No forecast for this night.

Point at the plot to read the values under the pointer on the line under it: the time, the target or Moon line nearest the pointer (airmass and altitude), the block at that time, and that hour's weather. Click a block to select its row in the table; the selected target is drawn bright and thick and every other one fades. Click beside the blocks to clear it. A legend under the plot repeats these meanings.

The plot grows with the window: a taller window gives a taller plot.

Note: If Matplotlib is missing, the plot's place shows ⚠ Night plot needs matplotlib — run Create shortcuts.bat again on Windows (on a Mac and on Linux it names that system's start file instead). Everything else on the page works. See chapter 10.

Notes you will see

  • The verdict (first, on every block that has one): the verdict word with its S/N, then what it rests on, worked out for this night's airmass and Moon (chapter 5):

  • Timed variables: Minimum at 01:14 or Maximum at 22:05.

  • Campaigns with a goal: Goal: 3 nights a week, and when it is behind, Behind its weekly goal (0 of 3 so far): moved up one priority level.

  • No-transit windows: No HD 219134 after 04:28 (planet c transit).

  • Flips (equatorial mount; see Flexible blocks and the meridian flip): Ends 3 min past its meridian, inside your tracking margin; stop by 03:38 at the latest (or Ends at its meridian; stop by 03:40 at the latest), Meridian flip at ~01:10; drop the frames during the flip, and Flips at the start (not run wholly before its meridian) so another target fits tonight. The flip-at-start note depends on the target's type (new in 1.4.0):

    • a variable or campaign: Start before the meridian and let it flip (~22:40). Pre-flip frames carry no weight in the night mean. One night's mean brightness does not need them.

  • Room for the next block: Ends 12 min early so DY Peg can start before its meridian.

  • Clouds: Cloud ≥ 70 % forecast for most of this block. A plain note, not a caution.

  • Your own frame interval: Frames every 45 s (your seconds between frames).

  • Your own notes from the target's Edit dialog.

Change a block

Select a row, then use the buttons under the table. A result line under the buttons says what happened.

  • Lock keeps the block's times through every rebuild (✔ HAT-P-23 b locked: it keeps its times when you rebuild.). The button reads Unlock for a locked block or one you set the times of.
  • Remove takes the block off this night (it comes back when you rebuild unless you untick it).
  • Move to night… moves a flexible block to another night of the schedule. It keeps its place in the sky, so it starts about 4 minutes earlier on the clock for each night later (later for an earlier night); the planner checks it is still up. The block is kept there (the table says set by you), and that night is rebuilt around it. A transit or a timed event cannot move: its time is set by the sky, so the button is grayed for those (should you get there anyway, the line says ✖ WASP-12 b happens at a time set by the sky (a transit or a timed event); it cannot move to another night.).
  • Edit times… asks for a new start and end (HH:MM on the site's clock). The block is marked set by you, keeps those times through rebuilds, and the night is rebuilt around it. The times must be between sunset and sunrise, and must not overlap a block that stays put (fixed, locked, set by you, or marked).

When the change leaves a problem, the result line says so with a ⚠, for example ⚠ BL Cam set by you: 23:10–01:00. Below your limits 00:40–01:00.

While a build or rebuild runs, these buttons wait: ⚠ Wait for the build to finish.

Flats and darks, didn't fit, free time

Under the table are three boxes.

Flats and darks to shoot. For example Flats: one set at the night's camera angle; a meridian flip's 180° turn needs no new flats, and Darks to shoot: 0.75 s, 45 s, 60 s. A defocused target adds flats at the defocus position too. List the darks you already have under Darks you already have in Setup's Suggestions and darks box (chapter 4), for example 60, 120, 180. Darks to shoot then lists only the exposures your library does not cover, and when it covers them all the line says Darks: all on hand (45 s, 60 s).

Why one set of flats is enough after a flip: a flip turns the camera 180° with the telescope, so the dust and vignetting stay where they were relative to the sensor. A rotator angle change is different, which is why the plan keeps the camera angle fixed.

Didn't fit, and why. Every ticked target that got no block, with the reason and its next chance, for example:

  • WASP-12 b: collides with HAT-P-23 b; next chance Fri 23 Oct
  • BL Cam: below your limits all night (highest 24°; limit 30° there); next chance Mon 26 Oct
  • DY Peg: no free stretch of 45 min left tonight (longest 19 min); next chance Wed 04 Nov
  • KELT-1 b: no transit tonight
  • BL Cam: Moon too close (38° at best; default for a gibbous Moon 50°), or for a transit Moon too close during the transit 22:10–00:45 (31°; your limit 40°). The words in brackets say which limit was used: your limit 40° is the target's own Moon at least; default for a gibbous Moon 50° (crescent, quarter, gibbous or full) is Setup's Moon distance at least by phase; Setup's default 30° is its Fixed value (chapter 4).

When another block took the room (a collision, or no free stretch left), a green Plan this instead button appears under the line. It rebuilds the night with that target first; the one that gave way then appears here instead. It only ever plans a ticked target. The result line says ✔ Rebuilt with WASP-12 b first., or ✖ Still didn't fit: … when it cannot be done.

Free time. Gaps of 15 minutes or more in the dark with nothing planned, for example Free 00:36–01:02 (26 min). A good place for an extra any-time variable, or for flats and darks.

Weather and dew

The forecast comes from Open-Meteo for your site each time you build or re-review. It is shown:

  • in the night's line (the summary),
  • in the weather strip over the night plot,
  • in the blocks' cautions (dew) and notes (cloud),
  • on the plan card.

Why dew matters for photometry: a dewing corrector or mirror dims the stars gradually, which looks like a slow trend in your light curve, or ruins a transit's egress. With End dew-sensitive runs at the predicted dew onset ticked in Setup, flexible blocks end at the dew onset (⚠ Dew expected from 04:05: the block ends there). Fixed-time blocks are not cut: a transit happens when it happens, so it gets a ⚠ instead, and you decide.

Cloudy hours are not removed from the plan. A block that is mostly under 70 % cloud or more gets the note Cloud ≥ 70 % forecast for most of this block. It is a plain note, not a ⚠, and it does not put a ⚠ on the night's tab: the plan stays, because forecasts change, and you decide on the night.

  2. Select a block.
  3. Choose its Status: Done, Partial or Clouded out. The empty entry at the top of the list clears a mark (✔ BL Cam: mark cleared (planned, not marked).).

Marked blocks stay where they are on rebuilds, and the table shows the mark. Done and Clouded out rows are grayed.

Re-review: roll the week forward

Re-review schedule moves the schedule on to tonight:

  1. Nights before tonight go into the history, with their marks.
  2. The schedule keeps its length (seven nights stays seven nights), so new nights are added at the end.
  3. Everything that is not locked, set by you or marked is rebuilt, with a new forecast. Targets clouded out or only partly observed are back in the pool and get planned again where they fit.

If locked blocks on past nights were never marked, SHOBS-SCHED asks first: they would go to the history as Planned, not Done. Mark them first if you observed them. Like Build schedule, it also asks about blocks you locked or set by hand whose targets are unticked or removed (see "Build a schedule" above).

The busy bar says Re-reviewing 7 nights… while it works, and the line under the controls reports the result as for a build.

Weekly goals for campaigns

A campaign with a Goal (nights a week, in its Edit dialog) is paced through the week, Monday to Sunday:

  • Done nights count, and so do nights already planned for it earlier in this schedule. Partial and Clouded out nights do not.
  • A campaign is behind when it cannot reach its goal without tonight, or is behind an even pace through the week. A goal of 1 is behind from Monday until it has a night; a goal of 2 is behind from Monday until it has one night, and from Thursday until it has two.
  • A campaign that is behind is placed one priority level higher. If there is no room at all, it may shorten other flexible blocks (toward their shortest useful length) to get its night.

The Targets page's This week column shows the progress, for example 2 of 3 this week.

Warnings about the schedule itself

  • Setup or targets changed. After you save a change in Setup, or change a ticked target (tick, untick, edit, priority), the schedule was built from the old values. The page says ⚠ Setup or targets changed since this schedule was built: press Build schedule (or Re-review) to rebuild it with the changes. The OBSPlan page shows a similar warning, and still lets you print and export. Unticked suggestions coming and going do not count.
  • The schedule starts before tonight. ⚠ The schedule starts on Mon 19 Oct, before tonight. Mark what happened and press Re-review schedule to roll it forward. Past nights stay visible as a record, with a ⚠ line above the plot: ⚠ Mon 19 Oct has passed: this tab is the record of that night (mark what happened, then Re-review schedule).
  • Every night has passed. ⚠ Last built for Thu 22 Oct (past): press Build schedule.

7. OBSPlan: the plan card, ASIAIR and NINA

The OBSPlan page is the plan card itself, on screen: the nights you tick, laid out the way they print. From here you print the card, and send one night at a time to ASIAIR or NINA.

Its subtitle reads: The plan card for the ticked nights. Print it, or send one night at a time to ASIAIR or NINA: their imports take the targets' positions only; times, exposures and notes stay on the card.

This chapter covers:

  • choosing the nights,
  • what is on the card,
  • printing, with or without the night plots,
  • Export to ASIAIR, Copy ASIAIR list and the ASIAIR export folder,
  • Export to NINA,
  • the camera angle in both lists.

Choose the nights

Under Nights is one tick per night of the schedule, with its number of blocks, for example Tue 20 Oct (3 blocks). Tonight and the nights after it are ticked at first; past nights are not. The card below shows the ticked nights; Print plan card prints them.

With no schedule yet, the page says No schedule yet: build one on the Schedule page.

What is on the card

The printed card's title is Photometry plan, Tue 20 Oct 2026 for one night, or Photometry plan, Tue 20 Oct to Mon 26 Oct 2026 for several. Under it is your site line: the site's name, its latitude and longitude, and your observer code if SHOBS-P gave one (Example Observatory · 40.000° N, 100.000° W).

For each night:

  • The date, with the dark hours and which darkness (Dark 19:42–05:58 (astronomical)), the Moon in the order the dark night meets it (Moon 62 %, down at dusk, rises 23:10, or Moon 95 %, up all night; the same words as the Schedule page, chapter 6), and which clock the times are on (Times: Local time (UTC−5)).
  • The weather: Weather (Open-Meteo): Clear until 02:00, then 60–80 % cloud. High 12 °C, low 4 °C, wind up to 15 km/h. ⚠ Dew from 04:05.
  • The night plot (when Include night plots is ticked; printed card only).
  • The nightly table: Time, Target, RA / Dec (J2000), Alt, Meridian, Exposure, Frames and Notes. The times are big and bold, so you can read them at the telescope. On screen each target's name sits on its type's color (the printed card is plain black on white). Locked blocks say (locked), blocks you timed say (set by you), and marked blocks show their mark in brackets ([Done]). Estimated exposures say est. (the Schedule page's table writes (est.)); an exposure chosen by S/N says (S/N) (120 s (S/N)) and never est. Under the exposure is the block's verdict word (new in 1.4.0; chapter 5): on screen on a green tint for Detectable and a yellow one for Marginal, on the printed card in bold. The verdict and its S/N also lead the block's notes. The Meridian column gives the time only; a flip during the block is in the notes.
  • Flats, Darks and Free time, as lists under the table. Under Darks, To shoot: … lists the exposures not covered by Setup's Darks you already have, or All on hand (…) when they all are.

The footer gives the clock once more, with the time zone's name: SHOBS-SCHED · all times Local time (UTC−5) (America/Chicago) on the printed card, All times Local time (UTC−5) (America/Chicago) on screen.

"Didn't fit, and why" is not on the card. That is a planning decision you make on the Schedule page; the card is what you will actually do.

Print the plan card

  1. Tick the nights you want.
  2. Leave Include night plots ticked to print each night's plot, or untick it for a shorter card. The choice is remembered.
  3. Press Print plan card.
  4. While the plots are drawn, the busy bar at the lower left says Drawing the night plots for the plan card… (with a count, 2 of 7, for several nights). Print plan card is grayed until it is done.
  5. The card opens in your web browser, and the result line says ✔ The plan card is open in your browser; print it from there. Print it from the browser (in most browsers, Ctrl+P).

The card is black on white with no colors you need, so it prints well on any printer. It is set up to print in landscape. With plots, each night takes two pages: the date, facts and plot on the first, the table and lists on the second. A table never splits across pages if it fits on one.

The card is saved as plan_card.html in SHOBS-SCHED's cache folder (chapter 2), replaced each time you print. If the browser does not open, the result line gives the file's full path so you can open it yourself.

Tip: Untick past nights before printing: the card is easier to use at the telescope with only the nights to come.

Export to ASIAIR

ASIAIR's Plan can import a list of targets. SHOBS-SCHED writes that list for one night at a time, in the order the blocks start, each target once. It carries the targets' positions (RA and Dec, J2000) and the camera angle. ASIAIR does not import times or exposures; those are on your plan card.

  1. Find the night on the card. Beside its date are Export to ASIAIR, Export to NINA and Copy ASIAIR list.
  2. Press Export to ASIAIR.
  3. A result line under the night's heading says where the file went: ✔ ASIAIR: saved 3 targets for Tue 20 Oct to C:\Users\you\Downloads\SHOBS-SCHED_ASIAIR_2026-10-20.csv. In ASIAIR's Plan: Add → Import, and paste the file's text. Position angle 0.00° (set your usual camera angle in Setup).
  4. Under the line are Open folder, Open in Notepad and Show file contents ▸ (which shows the file's text right there).
  5. In ASIAIR, open Plan, choose Add → Import, and paste the file's text.

The file is named SHOBS-SCHED_ASIAIR_<date>.csv. Its format is the one Telescopius gives ASIAIR (Pane, RA, DEC, Position Angle (East), Pane width (arcmins), Pane height (arcmins), Overlap, Row, Column), with each target's name in the first column, in plain letters.

The ASIAIR export folder

By default the files go to your Downloads folder. To have them land on the phone or tablet that runs ASIAIR:

  1. Under ASIAIR export folder:, press Choose folder….
  2. Pick a folder that syncs to that device, for example an iCloud Drive or OneDrive folder.
  3. The line says ✔ Export to ASIAIR now writes to …. Each night's file is then waiting on the device.

Use Downloads (shown once you have chosen a folder) goes back to the Downloads folder. If the chosen folder goes missing (a drive not connected), the folder line shows ⚠ not found: files go to Downloads, and the export says where the file went instead.

Copy ASIAIR list

Copy ASIAIR list puts the same list on the clipboard instead of in a file: ✔ Copied 3 targets for Tue 20 Oct. In ASIAIR's Plan: Add → Import, and paste. Position angle 0.00° (set your usual camera angle in Setup). Use it when ASIAIR runs on the same computer, or you paste through a remote-desktop app.

When some targets have no position, they are left out and the line says so with a ⚠, still with the ASIAIR steps: ⚠ Copied 2 targets for Tue 20 Oct; left out (no coordinates): DY Peg. In ASIAIR's Plan: Add → Import, and paste. Position angle 0.00° (set your usual camera angle in Setup).

Export to NINA

Export to NINA writes the night's targets for N.I.N.A.'s Sequencer, in the same Telescopius layout (proper CSV, with each target's real name). The file goes to your Downloads folder as SHOBS-SCHED_NINA_<date>.csv.

In NINA: Sequencer → Import targets, and pick the file. The result line warns you, as NINA's import does: It REPLACES the targets now in the sequence; each target gets your default sequence template. So set up your default template for photometry (exposure, filter, and so on) first, or adjust each target after the import.

As with ASIAIR, the file carries positions and the camera angle only.

The camera angle

Both lists give each target a position angle: Setup's Usual camera angle (rotator) (chapter 4), the same for every target. The line under Print plan card says what it is, for example ASIAIR and NINA lists: position angle 210.00° (Setup's usual camera angle).

Why it matters: ASIAIR reads the angle as "turn the camera to this angle". If it were left at 0° while your flats were taken at another angle, the plan would turn the camera and your flats would no longer match the frames. Set your usual angle in Setup (or read it from a FITS frame) so the plan keeps the camera where your flats were taken. When no angle is set, 0° is written and the line says set your usual camera angle in Setup.

When the plan is out of date

If Setup or your targets changed since the schedule was built, a ⚠ bar at the top of the page says ⚠ Plan out of date: Setup or targets changed since it was built. Press Build schedule on the Schedule page before printing or exporting. Printing and exports still work, but each result line starts with ⚠ Plan out of date (Setup or targets changed since the build). A result for a night disappears when that night is rebuilt, since the file no longer matches it.

Some things to know:

  • A multi-step job changes its words step by step. Build schedule, for example, first says (when there are comps to check) Checking comp stars for …, then Getting the forecast from Open-Meteo…, then Building 7 nights…, then Working out twilight and Moon times….
  • When the count is known, the words carry it. A suggestion search says how far it has got, … 12 of 40. The bar itself still moves back and forth; it never fills up.
  • A search in two passes names each pass, and each counts up on its own. Find suggested transits… first works out the transits of every planet in the Archive's list (Finding suggested transits: transits of each planet… 291 of 302), then checks the best of them again with each planet's most precise ephemeris (Finding suggested transits: the best with their most precise ephemeris… 12 of 50).
  • Dialogs have their own busy bar. The Edit dialog's planet lookup, for example, shows its busy bar at the dialog's lower left (Looking up HD 219134's planets at the NASA Exoplanet Archive…, or Asking APASS (VizieR) for comparison stars… after Check comps), so the main window stays free.
  • You can keep working. Lookups, searches and builds run in the background; the window stays responsive. The only thing that waits is the schedule itself: while a build runs, Build schedule and Re-review schedule are grayed out, and changes to a night's blocks say ⚠ Wait for the build to finish.

When the job is done, the busy bar disappears first (unless another job is still running), and then the result appears, so the job's last words never sit beside its result.

Result lines: ✔, ⚠ and ✖

When a job finishes, a short result line appears next to the button you pressed: beside Add on the Targets page, under the night's heading on the OBSPlan page, and so on. Build schedule and Re-review schedule put their result only on the line under the Schedule page's controls (✔ Built 7 nights: 11 blocks.), not beside the button. 9. Credits and citing

SHOBS-SCHED stands on other people's work: catalogs and online services, a published plot design, Python libraries and published methods. When you publish results from nights planned with it, please credit the sources your work used.

  2. At the top: SHOBS-SCHED 1.4.1 © 2026 Shiloh Hill Observatory, the license line, and How to cite SHOBS-SCHED with the citation and a Copy button (see "How to cite SHOBS-SCHED" below).

The night plot and TAPIR

The Schedule page's night plot follows the design of the airmass plot in TAPIR (plot_airmass.cgi) by Eric Jensen, Swarthmore College: Jensen, E. 2013, Tapir: A web interface for transit/eclipse observability, Astrophysics Source Code Library, ascl:1306.007; <https://github.com/elnjensen/Tapir> (GPL-3). The ideas taken from it: airmass and elevation axes, twilight shading with the times across the top, the nautical-twilight ± 1.5 h time span, and the Moon's line growing with its phase. SHOBS-SCHED's plot is its own code (Matplotlib); no TAPIR code is copied.

The self-test

The self-test checks SHOBS-SCHED with the same Python the shortcuts use. It changes nothing of yours: it works in a temporary folder and deletes it at the end.

  1. Open the SHOBS-SCHED program folder.
  2. Double-click Run self-test.bat (on a Mac, Run self-test.command; on Linux, type bash start_linux.sh selftest in a terminal in that folder). A console window opens.
  3. Wait for it to finish (usually under a minute).
  4. Read the last result line. It should be ALL PASSED.

The sections:

  • 1. Environment: your Python, NumPy, Matplotlib (with its Tk part, for the night plot), Tk, the time zones, and the time-zone guess.
  • 2. Sky and events: dark times, transit times, a no-transit window and a meridian time, against a known week of hand-made plans (each within a few minutes).
  • 3. Planner: those five nights built again (every target placed, no overlaps), and a seven-night build.
  • 4. Weather and export: a recorded forecast, the nightly table, the ASIAIR list and the plan card.
  • 4b. Exposure by S/N (new in 1.4.0): the shared shobs_profile.py file is the version SHOBS-SCHED was built with; with no SHOBS-FLUX profile the numbers are a rough estimate, and the cold start predicts the same peak as 1.3.0; the example SHOBS-FLUX profile is found; rough numbers keep a transit on Longest safe; TOI-2046 b by S/N gives Detectable?; a saturating exposure says Saturates?; the Moon on a measured sky; on the cold start Longest safe is 1.3.0's rule; with a profile, Longest safe never says Saturates; a zero point typed in Setup gives Best precision and one peak model; the flip notes by type; SHOBS-P's DOI.
  • 5. Window: the app opened on a temporary folder with the network switched off: every page, a build, and the ASIAIR list copied. Without a screen it says SKIP.
  • 6. Live services (information only): whether the NASA Exoplanet Archive, SIMBAD, VizieR (VSX copy), SIMBAD mirror (IDIA), VizieR mirror (IDIA), AAVSO VSX and Open-Meteo answer. Each is OK or NOTE; never a failure. A NOTE says did not answer (…); SCHED falls back to its saved copies. Then the two Priority lists of Find suggested transits…: OK ExoClock planet list answered: … planets and OK ExoFOP TOI table answered: … TOIs (not FP/FA), or a NOTE ending Find suggested transits searches without it. (The ExoFOP table is large; this check can take a minute.)

Each check is PASS or FAIL. If the end says FAILED: N check(s): …, the lines marked FAIL say which.

The log goes to selftest_log.txt in the program folder and a copy to SHOBS-SCHED_selftest_log.txt in your Downloads folder. Attach it to any problem report. On a Mac, Run self-test.command says where both are when it finishes. If it says The self-test did not pass or could not run, leave the window open and copy the message above it; when no Python could be set up, the test never ran and there is no log, so that message is all there is.

The program's log

SHOBS-SCHED started from its icon has no console window. It writes what it did, and every error in full, to sched.log in the .shobs_sched folder in your user folder (C:\Users\<you>\.shobs_sched\sched.log), each line with the date and time. Lookups, builds, saved files and fallbacks to saved copies are all there. When the log grows past 2 MB it is renamed sched.log.1 and a new one starts.

If something goes wrong inside the window, the status strip says ✖ Something went wrong: … Details are in …\sched.log. Open the log, and look at the last lines.

The folder name starts with a dot. On Windows, type %USERPROFILE%\.shobs_sched into the Explorer address bar to open it. On Linux or a Mac it is ~/.shobs_sched.

On a Mac, when SHOBS-SCHED does not start. If Start SHOBS-SCHED.command fails, its Terminal window stays open and says SHOBS-SCHED did not start. Leave this window open and copy the message above. It then names the two logs: The package install log is ~/Library/Application Support/SHOBS-SCHED/pip_log.txt; the program's own log is ~/.shobs_sched/sched.log. Send both with your report. (In Finder, Go → Go to Folder… and paste the path.)

"Night plot needs matplotlib"

If a night tab shows ⚠ Night plot needs matplotlib — … where the plot should be, Matplotlib is not installed in SHOBS-SCHED's environment (for example after updating from a version that did not need it). The message names the fix for your system:

System The message ends What to do
Windows — run Create shortcuts.bat again Close SHOBS-SCHED, double-click Create shortcuts.bat (it installs any missing package), and start SHOBS-SCHED again.
Mac — start SHOBS-SCHED again with Start SHOBS-SCHED.command (it installs what is missing) Close SHOBS-SCHED and double-click Start SHOBS-SCHED.command.

  • Linux: install your Python's Tkinter package (e.g. python3-tk).

When the online services do not answer

SHOBS-SCHED asks five services, each with its own fallback. VizieR and SIMBAD each have mirrors that serve the same data, and SHOBS-SCHED asks them in turn:

Service Used for When it does not answer
NASA Exoplanet Archive Planets, ephemerides, the transit list, a star's planets The saved copy is used (with a ⚠ saying how old). A planet never looked up before cannot be added until it answers.
AAVSO VSX Variable stars VizieR's copy of VSX is asked instead; if that fails too, the saved copy.
VizieR (CDS) VSX's copy; the variable-star search VizieR's other service at CDS is asked, then its mirrors at the CfA and at IDIA (Cape Town); if none answers, the saved copy, if any.
SIMBAD (CDS) Positions of other stars Its mirror at IDIA (Cape Town) is asked; if that fails too, the saved copy. With neither, a planet host star's position is taken from the NASA Exoplanet Archive.
Open-Meteo The forecast The last saved forecast (with its age); else the plan is built without weather.

Every failure is said in plain words, with the reason, for example service unavailable, HTTP 503, no answer within 30 s, no network connection (name lookup failed), or the service's firewall (Cloudflare) blocked the query. When SIMBAD and its mirror both fail, both reasons are given: …; its mirror: ….

SHOBS-SCHED tells a service that does not answer from one that does not know the name:

  • SIMBAD does not know HD 12345. Check the spelling, or type the coordinates by hand. SIMBAD answered: check the name.
  • SIMBAD did not answer (…) and there is no saved copy for HD 12345. Try again later. SIMBAD (and its mirror) could not be reached, or reported a problem on its side: the name may be fine.

CDS skipped for 10 minutes (1.4.1). When a CDS server (VizieR or SIMBAD at cds.unistra.fr) does not answer, SCHED skips CDS for the next 10 minutes and goes straight to the mirrors, instead of waiting out CDS's timeout on every lookup. The first lookup after that tries CDS again. The log says CDS did not answer (…); skipping CDS for 10 min (mirrors first) and later CDS answered again; no longer skipped. A reply that comes back with an error page (such as page not found) does not start the skip: CDS answered.

The plan itself never needs the network: once your targets are in the list, you can build offline. A Build does ask APASS first for the comp candidates of targets that have no saved comp check (chapter 5, "The three bounds"); offline, those targets are planned on the target star alone, with the note comps not checked: catalog unreachable; check comp peaks in SHOBS-P.

ExoClock and ExoFOP (Find suggested transits' Priority lists)

These two are asked only when you choose a Priority in Find suggested transits… (chapter 5), and each list is saved for a week.

  • The server does not answer, or the list needs a login now. The search goes on without that list and says so with a ⚠ (⚠ ExoFOP did not answer (…) — searched without its priorities.). With a saved copy, that copy is used (…; using the saved copy from 3 days ago). Nothing for you to fix: try again later. ExoFOP's table is large (about 4 MB) and its server can be slow; SHOBS-SCHED waits up to two minutes for it.
  • ✖ … answered, but its list is not in the form SCHED knows (a format change or a SCHED bug; please report it). The site changed its list, or SHOBS-SCHED has a bug. This is never hidden, because a quietly wrong list would give wrong suggestions. Please report it (below), with the log. Until it is fixed, untick that list's ExoClock levels (or set ExoFOP SG1 to Don't use) and the search runs without it.

Typical problems

Problem Likely cause Fix
All times are off by some hours. The time zone on the Setup page is wrong (or still the guess from a computer in another zone). Pick your site's zone and Save.
Times are right, but the target is in the wrong part of the sky. Longitude has the wrong sign. West longitudes are negative (-100.0).
A transit you expected says none in the next 7 nights. Not all of it is in the dark and above your limits on those nights. Look at more nights, lower Minimum altitude if your sky allows, or check the horizon profile.
A target never gets a block. Read its line under Didn't fit, and why on the night's tab. The reason says what to change (limits, Moon distance, its own window, priority).
✖ No dark time tonight: the Sun gets no lower than … in summer. The Sun never reaches −18°. If the line goes on nautical darkness (Sun below −12°) is possible if you allow it in Setup, choose Nautical (Sun −12°) in Setup.
Exposures look much too short or too long. Missing or wrong telescope or camera values, or the linear limit. Check Setup; or choose My exposure in the target's Edit dialog and type your own.
Every verdict ends in "?" (Detectable?, Out of reach?). No measured zero point: no SHOBS-FLUX profile was found, or it has not measured one yet. That is expected without SHOBS-FLUX. Check Setup's Photometry noise line; choose your profile there if Automatic does not find it. Typing a zero point you trust also removes the "?".
Transits are on Longest safe, not Best precision. Best precision is a transit's default only once the zero point is measured by SHOBS-FLUX or typed in Setup's Photometry noise box. Type a zero point in Setup, or choose Best precision for the target in its Edit dialog.
Setup says ⚠ Several SHOBS-FLUX camera profiles fit … SHOBS-FLUX has a camera profile for more than one gain, and Setup has no Gain setting. Choose the profile in Instrument profile, or type the Gain setting, and Save.
Setup says ⚠ A newer SHOBS-FLUX wrote … profiles here SHOBS-FLUX is newer than this SHOBS-SCHED. Install the newest SHOBS-SCHED.
A target is Out of reach but you have measured it before. A rough zero point, or a wrong seeing or sky value, can make the S/N too low; or the transit's depth or the star's VSX range is wrong. The window does not open from the icon. Python or a package problem. Start run_windows.bat and read the console.
Setup changes "did not stick". They were saved, but the schedule was built before. Press Build schedule (the ⚠ line says so).
Find suggested transits… finds few planets, or none. The filters in Setup's Suggestions and darks box (host V range, depth, baseline, Moon-brightened sky) are tight for your nights, or Hide out of reach is ticked. The Find suggested transits pop-up shows the values it uses. Widen them in Setup and Save, or untick Hide out of reach, then Find again.
Darks to shoot lists exposures you already have. Darks you already have in Setup's Suggestions and darks box is empty or missing them. Type them there (for example 60, 120, 180) and Save, then Build schedule.
On untick, Remove, Build schedule or Re-review schedule, a question Blocks set by hand comes up. A target that is no longer planned still has blocks you locked or set by hand; a rebuild keeps those. A target often says Moon too close in Didn't fit, even with the Moon well away from where you would worry. Find suggested transits… finds fewer planets than in 1.2.0. 1.3.0 also keeps suggestions clear of the Moon by Setup's Moon distance at least; or a Priority is chosen, so only listed planets are searched. Check the window's text (it names the Moon distance) and the Priority rows.
Every equatorial block suddenly flips at its start. Tracks past the meridian was saved as 0 by an older version (an empty field once saved 0). Type your mount's real value (5 by default) and Save. Since 1.3.0 an empty field keeps the saved value.
SHOBS-P's site does not appear. The fields were typed in SHOBS-SCHED (tagged set here), which win. Type SHOBS-P's value back into the field and Save: it follows SHOBS-P again.

Starting afresh

The Python environment. If the install gets into a muddle, delete the SHOBS-SCHED folder in %LOCALAPPDATA% and run Create shortcuts.bat again. On a Mac, delete ~/Library/Application Support/SHOBS-SCHED; on Linux, ~/.local/share/SHOBS-SCHED; then start SHOBS-SCHED with its start file, which sets it up again. Your settings, targets and schedule are not in that folder, so they are kept.

Your data. To start with an empty Setup, target list or schedule, close SHOBS-SCHED and rename (or delete) the file in your user folder: .shobs_sched_settings.json, .shobs_sched_targets.json, or .shobs_sched\schedule.json. Renaming keeps a copy in case you want it back.

11. Glossary

Terms used in SHOBS-SCHED and in this manual, in alphabetical order (new in this edition: the words of the exposure by S/N, such as verdict, zero point, noise budget and scintillation). Words in bold inside an entry have their own entry.

Airmass — How much atmosphere a star's light passes through: 1 at the zenith, about 1.15 at 60° altitude, 2 at 30°. Stars dim, redden and twinkle more with airmass, and the change is fastest low down. The night plot's left axis is airmass; Setup's Maximum airmass is a limit.

Alt-az mount — A mount that moves in altitude and azimuth. It never makes a meridian flip, but cannot track smoothly through the zenith (see zenith keyhole). Choose Alt-az under Mount in Setup.

Any-time variable — A variable star with no single moment worth catching: a pulsator whose whole cycle is useful, or a slow long-period star. Planned as a flexible block at its best airmass.

APASS — The AAVSO Photometric All-Sky Survey: V and B−V of stars from about V 7 to 17. Check comps in a target's Edit dialog asks its catalog (DR9, at VizieR) for comparison stars.

Band — The color of light a measurement is made in. SHOBS-SCHED's main band is a color camera's green channel (TG) or a mono camera's clear band (CV); a SHOBS-FLUX profile may add others (red TR, blue TB, B, V, R, I, Sloan g′ r′ i′ z′ u′). The Edit dialog's Band drop-down picks one for the S/N.

Baseline — In transit work, the steady out-of-transit light before ingress and after egress. The fit needs it to know the star's normal brightness and its slow trends. SHOBS-SCHED plans 60 minutes a side by default, trimmed to no less than 20 when two targets share a night.

Best precision — One of the three ways to choose a target's exposure (Edit dialog, Exposure): the shortest standard exposure within 2 % of the best precision per 10-minute bin whose peak stays under 80 % of the linear limit. A transit's default once your zero point is measured by SHOBS-FLUX or typed in Setup. Shown as (S/N) after the exposure on the Schedule page and plan card.

BJD_TDB — Barycentric Julian Date in Barycentric Dynamical Time: a time stamp corrected to the center of mass of the Solar System, the standard for transit times. A transit's Tc is given in it.

Blackout — See no-transit window.

Block — A stretch of the night given to one target. A fixed block's time is set by the sky (a transit, a timed minimum or maximum); a flexible block is placed where the target is best.

Campaign — A star or field you come back to on many nights, with an optional weekly goal and rules of its own. One of the three target types (plum).

Comparison star (comp) — A steady star in the same field that the target is measured against, so changes in the sky cancel out. Good comps are about as bright as the target and of about the same color. See APASS.

Composite ephemeris — The NASA Exoplanet Archive's planet-list ephemeris for a planet. It can be years old; SHOBS-SCHED replaces it with the most precise ephemeris for planets you plan.

Darkness — When the Sun is low enough to plan in: astronomical darkness (Sun below −18°) or nautical (below −12°). Chosen under Darkness to plan in in Setup.

Depth — How much a star dims during a transit, in percent (or as a fraction): 1 % means the star loses one hundredth of its light. Deeper transits are easier to detect.

Dew point, spread — The dew point is the temperature at which moisture condenses. The spread is the air temperature minus the dew point; when it falls under Setup's threshold (2.5 °C by default), dew on the optics is likely.

Duration (T14) — The length of a transit from ingress to egress.

Eclipsing binary — Two stars orbiting each other in our line of sight, so each eclipses the other. Their minima happen at predictable times; timing them shows whether the period is changing. VSX types EA, EB, EW.

Egress — The end of a transit, when the planet has left the star's disk.

Ephemeris — A planet's (or a variable star's) period and a reference time (Tc or epoch), from which every future event is predicted. Its uncertainty grows with every cycle away from the reference time.

ExoClock — A project (www.exoclock.space) that keeps the transit times of known planets up to date for ESA's Ariel space mission, with help from amateur and professional observers. It ranks its planets Alert, High, Medium or Low by how badly new timings are needed. Find suggested transits… can search only the levels you tick (its Priority rows).

ExoFOP (ExoFOP-TESS) — The Exoplanet Follow-up Observing Program website for TESS, run by NASA's Exoplanet Science Institute at Caltech. Its TOI table lists the TESS planet candidates with their ephemerides and the follow-up groups' priorities (see SG1 priority).

Exposure estimate — SHOBS-SCHED's first guess of a good exposure for a star, from its V magnitude, your aperture, camera and the seeing: the longest standard exposure that keeps the star's peak under about 80 % of your linear limit. This is the Longest safe rule while the numbers are rough. Marked (est.) in the Schedule page's table and est. on the plan card.

Extinction — How much the atmosphere dims a star, in magnitudes per airmass: about 0.2 in V at a low site. In Setup's Photometry noise box.

Fixed-time target — A target whose block is set by the sky: a transit, or a timed minimum or maximum. Planned before the flexible ones.

Flats — Frames of an evenly lit field, used to correct dust shadows and vignetting. They must be taken at the same camera angle (and focus) as the lights.

Flexible block — A block placed where its target is best (near its meridian), of the target's Block length. Campaigns and any-time variables get flexible blocks. On an equatorial mount it runs wholly before the meridian when a full block fits there, otherwise it starts just before the meridian (see meridian flip).

Goal — A campaign's wanted number of Done nights a week (Monday to Sunday). A campaign behind its goal is planned one priority level higher.

Ingress — The start of a transit, when the planet starts to cross the star's disk.

Instrument profile — A SHOBS-FLUX file that describes one telescope-and-camera system with what was measured about it. Chosen in Setup's Photometry noise box (Automatic finds the one for your telescope and camera).

J2000 — The standard reference frame for star positions (RA and Dec), fixed at the start of the year 2000.

Linear limit — The raw brightness (in ADU) above which your camera's response is no longer proportional to the light. A star whose brightest pixel goes past it is measured too faint. Set it in Setup (or let a SHOBS-FLUX profile give it); the exposure estimate keeps star peaks under it, and a predicted peak over it makes the verdict Saturates.

Longest safe — One of the three ways to choose a target's exposure (Edit dialog, Exposure): the longest standard exposure whose predicted peak stays under 80 % of the linear limit — 1.3.0's rule (the exposure estimate) with no SHOBS-FLUX profile and nothing typed in Setup, otherwise the noise model's peak. The default for variables and campaigns, and for transits until the zero point is measured by SHOBS-FLUX or typed in Setup.

Meridian — The north–south line through the zenith. A star is highest (lowest airmass) when it crosses the meridian.

Meridian flip — On a German equatorial mount, the turn to the other side of the pier when a target crosses the meridian. It costs a few minutes and moves the star on the sensor, so a flip in the middle of a run leaves a step in the light curve. For a flexible block SHOBS-SCHED avoids the flip when it can: the block runs wholly before the meridian, ending inside your tracking margin. When a full block does not fit there, or when running it there would leave another target without a block that night, the block starts about 12 minutes before the meridian, so the flip happens at the start. A flip in the middle of a flexible run is a last resort, with a ⚠.

Minimum, maximum — The faintest or brightest point in a variable star's cycle. An eclipsing binary's minimum or an RR Lyrae star's maximum can be timed to minutes.

Mirror — A copy of a service on another server. When SIMBAD or VizieR at CDS does not answer, SHOBS-SCHED asks a mirror (VizieR at the CfA, SIMBAD and VizieR at IDIA in Cape Town).

Moon distance (Moon at least) — The closest the Moon may come to a target while the Moon is up, in degrees. A target's own Moon at least (Edit dialog) wins; otherwise Setup's Moon distance at least applies, which by default goes by the Moon phase that night (crescent 20°, quarter 35°, gibbous 50°, full 70°), or is one fixed value, or off.

Moon penalty — How much the Moon brightens the sky at a target, in magnitudes, from the Moon's phase, height and distance (Krisciunas & Schaefer 1991). A brighter sky means more noise. Suggestions are limited by Setup's Moon-brightened sky up to (3 magnitudes by default).

Moon phase (for the Moon distance) — How much of the Moon is lit at the middle of the night: under 30 % is a crescent, under 70 % a quarter, under 95 % gibbous, and 95 % or more full. Waxing and waning count the same.

Most precise ephemeris — Of all the ephemerides published for a planet, the one with the smallest predicted Tc uncertainty for the coming weeks. SHOBS-SCHED uses it for planets you add, plan or keep.

My exposure — The third way to choose a target's exposure (Edit dialog, Exposure): your own, in seconds. It always wins.

Noise budget — Where the noise of a measurement comes from, as shares: photon noise, sky, scintillation and read + dark (the camera's read noise and dark current). Shown as a small colored bar in the Edit dialog's Exposure section.

No-transit window (blackout) — A time when a campaign or variable must not be observed, because one of its star's planets is transiting. Set in the target's Edit dialog.

O − C — Observed minus calculated: how much an observed event (a transit, a minimum) differs from the ephemeris' prediction. A trend in O − C shows a changing period; new timings of poorly known planets are valuable for this reason.

Period — The time between successive transits of a planet, or one cycle of a variable star.

Photon noise — The randomness of light itself: a star that gives N electrons in a frame scatters by about √N. The more light you collect, the smaller it is compared with the signal. Usually the largest part of the noise budget for faint stars.

Pixel scale — Arcseconds of sky per camera pixel: 206.265 × pixel size (µm) × binning ÷ focal length (mm).

Plan card — The printed plan: for each night, the dark hours, Moon, weather, night plot, nightly table, flats, darks and free time. Made on the OBSPlan page.

Position angle (camera angle) — The angle of the camera on the sky. Written to the ASIAIR and NINA lists from Setup's Usual camera angle.

ppt (parts per thousand) — A thousandth of the star's light: 1 ppt = 0.1 % = about 0.0011 mag. Precisions and the depths of shallow transits are often given in ppt.

Precision per 10-minute bin — The scatter you can expect in the average of 10 minutes of frames, in ppt. SHOBS-SCHED gives every precision this way, so short and long exposures can be compared fairly. It is for the target star alone (the comparison stars add a little more).

Priority — High, Normal or Low. Decides the order flexible targets are placed in, and which of two colliding fixed-time targets wins. Not to be confused with the Priority rows of Find suggested transits…, which are ExoClock and ExoFOP priorities: which planets those projects want timed.

Read noise — The noise a camera adds each time it reads a frame, in electrons. It matters most for short exposures and faint stars.

Rough estimate — An S/N or verdict worked out without a measured zero point (no SHOBS-FLUX profile, or one that has not measured it yet). The verdict word then ends in "?" (Detectable?), and the S/N in the notes has a "~" (S/N ~58). Same limits as a measured one, but a guess.

Saturation — A pixel at its maximum value; its light is lost. Saturation happens above the linear limit.

Scintillation — The twinkling of stars, made by moving air high above the telescope. It is larger for small telescopes, short exposures and high airmass, and smaller at high sites. Setup's Scintillation C_Y says how strong it is at your site (1.5 is typical; Young 1967, Osborn et al. 2015).

SG1 priority — The priority (1 = highest … 5) that TESS follow-up Sub Group 1 (seeing-limited ground-based photometry) gives a TOI in ExoFOP's table. SHOBS-SCHED reads its SG1B column. In Find suggested transits…, ExoFOP SG1 priority 1–2 searches the TOIs of priority 1 or 2.

SHOBS-FLUX — The SHOBS program that measures your telescope and camera (zero point, extinction, sky, seeing, scintillation, read noise, gain, linear limit) and keeps the results in instrument profiles in the .shobs folder. SHOBS-SCHED reads those profiles for the S/N (tag from FLUX) and never writes them.

Signal-to-noise (S/N) — How clearly a signal stands out from the noise. For a transit, its depth divided by the noise of the whole transit (the precision per 10-minute bin ÷ √(bins in transit)); for a variable, its VSX range divided by the precision of one 10-minute bin. 10 or more is Detectable, 5 to 10 Marginal, under 5 Out of reach (see verdict; chapter 5 has a worked example). Find suggested transits also ranks its suggestions by a simpler S/N of its own.

Site's clock — The clock in your site's time zone, with its daylight-saving changes. Every time in SHOBS-SCHED is on it, labeled for example Local time (UTC−5).

Tc (T0, mid-transit time) — The moment the planet is in the middle of its transit. The ephemeris' reference time is one Tc; Tc ± is the predicted uncertainty of a future one.

TOI (TESS Object of Interest) — A TESS planet candidate, named like TOI-1234.01. Many are not yet in the NASA Exoplanet Archive's planet list; SHOBS-SCHED then takes their ephemeris, depth and duration from ExoFOP, and estimates V from the TESS magnitude (V ≈ TESS mag + 0.5). Those that turned out false positives or false alarms are left out.

Tracking margin — How long your equatorial mount can track past the meridian before it must flip: Setup's Tracks past the meridian (5 minutes by default). A block that ends inside it needs no flip; its note says when to stop at the latest (stop by 23:47 at the latest).

Transit — A planet passing in front of its star, dimming it by the transit's depth for its duration. One of the three target types (green).

Twilight — Civil (Sun 0° to −6°), nautical (−6° to −12°) and astronomical (−12° to −18°). The night plot shades each band.

Variable — A star whose brightness changes, from AAVSO VSX. One of the three target types (navy).

Verdict — SHOBS-SCHED's one-word answer to "can my telescope measure this?": Detectable (S/N 10 or more, green), Marginal (5 to 10, yellow), Out of reach (under 5) or Saturates (the predicted peak is over the linear limit). With a "?" it is a rough estimate. Shown on the Targets page, in the Edit dialog, in the Schedule page's notes and on the plan card.

V magnitude — A star's brightness in the visual (V) band. Smaller is brighter; 5 magnitudes is a factor of 100.

VSX — The AAVSO International Variable Star Index: names, types, periods and epochs of variable stars.

Zenith keyhole — On an alt-az mount, the region near the zenith that the mount cannot track through smoothly. SHOBS-SCHED warns when a target passes above 85°.

Zero point — The magnitude of a star that gives one electron a second through your telescope and camera, in a band, with the star overhead. The key number of the S/N: from it come the star's and the sky's electrons. Measured by SHOBS-FLUX; without it, SHOBS-SCHED uses a rough estimate.
